# Mock 08 — Examiner script

Keep this file away from candidates. Spoken dialogue is labelled by role. Do not speak role labels or the --- break marker. Read continuously and naturally; do not emphasise answers. The marker divides questions 1–5/6–10 within Parts 1–3; pause 30 seconds there. Part 4 has no midpoint break. Full procedure: ../ADMINISTRATION.md.

## Listening

### Part 1 — Joining a neighbourhood walking group

Volunteer: Hello, Green Routes walking group. You’re speaking to Max.
Caller: Hello. I’d like to join one of your weekend walks. I saw a leaflet at the health centre, but it listed several groups and I wasn’t sure which one was suitable. I walk to work, though I haven’t done anything long in the countryside recently.
Volunteer: Our introductory route would be a sensible start. It’s mostly level and has places to stop. Could I take your name first? We keep a list so that the leader knows whom to expect, but we don’t ask for medical details on the telephone.
Caller: It’s Joel Ashby. That’s A-S-H-B-Y. My cousin has the same surname with an extra letter, so I usually spell it. Is there an annual membership payment?
Volunteer: There is for the longer programme, but your first walk costs nothing. You can decide about joining afterwards. The next introductory one is on Sunday. We used to run it on Saturday, but the leader now works then.
Caller: Sunday is better for me anyway. Where do you meet? The leaflet mentioned the station.
Volunteer: That was the starting point while the footbridge was being repaired. We’re back at the museum now, beside its main doors. It’s easy to find from the bus stop, and there’s shelter if people arrive early.
Caller: And how early is early? I prefer not to rush off a bus straight into a long walk.
Volunteer: We gather at nine fifteen and leave at nine thirty. Put 9:15 in your diary, because the leader gives a short briefing before departure. If you’re delayed, call the number on the confirmation rather than trying to catch up alone.
Caller: How far will we go? I can manage four kilometres comfortably, but eight might be a bit much the first time.
Volunteer: This one is six kilometres. There’s a shorter return option if someone needs it, although most people complete the full route. We don’t use the speed of the fastest person to set the pace. The leader stays aware of the group and a second volunteer walks at the back.
---
Caller: I own ordinary trainers. Would I need to buy boots before coming?
Volunteer: Not for this route if it’s dry. Trainers with a good grip are fine. Please bring a waterproof, though. A forecast can change, and the riverside section has nowhere to shelter. A large umbrella is awkward on the narrow path, so a jacket is more useful.
Caller: Should I bring lunch as well?
Volunteer: A snack and some water. We finish near a café, but buying anything there is entirely optional. Carry a bottle rather than depending on places along the way, since the public fountain is currently out of use.
Caller: I sometimes use a walking pole. Is the path wide enough for that?
Volunteer: Yes. There’s one section with a stile, but we use a nearby gate instead. We don’t go up the steps shown on an older route map. If you have any concerns about the gate width or surface, the leader can discuss them before the day.
Caller: That sounds reassuring. Can people bring dogs? My neighbour might come, and hers is very calm.
Volunteer: Dogs aren’t permitted on this particular walk because we cross a field containing livestock. Some of our other routes welcome them. It isn’t a general judgement about well-behaved dogs; it’s an agreement with the farmer for this route.
Caller: Fair enough. What happens if there’s severe weather? Would you call everyone?
Volunteer: We send a text by eight that morning if we cancel. For ordinary rain the walk usually goes ahead, but strong wind near the trees is different. Please reply to acknowledge a cancellation so we know you won’t travel unnecessarily. I’ll send a confirmation now with the route description and the leader’s contact number.

### Part 2 — An orientation talk at a community arts centre

Director: Welcome to everyone starting a course at the Foundry Arts Centre. The building was once a small factory, but its new name isn’t an invitation to operate industrial equipment. We run practical arts courses for adults, and some family sessions during school holidays. Today I’ll describe five activities you may want to try and then explain how the centre works. You don’t have to decide everything this morning. The aim is to help you understand which course fits the time and experience you actually have.
First, bookbinding. This starts next week and lasts four Tuesday evenings. Materials are provided, but we require a reservation because the tutor prepares a separate pack for each person. Turning up on the first evening and hoping to find an empty chair won’t work, even if someone is absent. We can often offer a place on the next course instead.
The lunchtime sketch group is quite different. There is no advance booking and no expectation that you already draw well. Its special arrangement is that it’s held outside the centre, in the covered market across the road. The group meets under the market clock. This gives people moving subjects to draw, and traders have agreed that participants may sit along the wall without blocking customers.
For textile printing, you should bring something of your own to print on. A plain cotton bag is ideal; a smooth T-shirt is another possibility. We provide inks and tools, but not the fabric item itself. Please avoid a favourite expensive garment for your first experiment. The tutor will explain why different surfaces absorb colour differently.
Our percussion session has been moved to an earlier time. People used to arrive at seven on Wednesdays; it now begins at six, because the neighbouring room is needed for a quiet class later in the evening. That’s a permanent change for this term, not just for the first week.
Finally, the clay modelling course allows children to attend, provided an adult takes the course with them. It isn’t a supervised drop-off club. Both people make something and can help each other. If you were hoping to work completely on your own, choose the adults-only session on the other day.
---
Now for some general information. Membership fees cover the shared studio space and basic maintenance. They don’t cover every course material. We used to charge one inclusive fee, but someone taking only sketching was helping pay for expensive clay. Separating the costs makes the arrangement more transparent, even though the price list is slightly longer.
Our noticeboard displays examples of work, but you don’t have to agree to public display. Some people enjoy sharing a first attempt; others want to practise privately. Ask the tutor to mark your work as not for display. That decision will have no effect on your access to future courses or the amount of help you receive.
Equipment may stay in the studio between sessions, but unfinished pieces must have a name and date. Unlabelled work is difficult to store responsibly because staff can’t tell whether it has been abandoned. We won’t throw things away immediately. After a published storage period, we’ll contact the maker where possible and clear space for current classes.
The café next door offers a small discount to course members on weekday afternoons. It is independent of the centre, so please raise food orders with café staff rather than a tutor. We mention it because many students like to continue talking after class, not because a café purchase is part of membership.
Please tell us if you need adjustments to take part. The best time is when you book, because some arrangements require planning. However, if you discover a difficulty during a session, speak to the tutor then. We don’t expect you to predict every problem in advance. The office can discuss an alternative rather than simply telling you to wait until the next term.

### Part 3 — Reviewing an interview project

Tutor: Come in, Amira and Ben. I’ve read your proposal about independent shopkeepers. You’ve found an interesting question about how businesses adapt when a street changes, but I think we need to look closely at recruitment.
Amira: We’ve made a list from the traders' association website. It seemed better than walking along the street and approaching whoever wasn’t busy.
Tutor: It’s organised, certainly. But remember that the association includes only members. Shops that closed recently won’t appear, and new businesses may not have joined. If you use it alone, you’ll hear from a particular surviving group.
Ben: We could add names from older street directories and contact the former owners where details are public. We can’t guarantee they’ll agree, but at least the recruitment frame wouldn’t automatically exclude them.
Amira: Should we ask the association chair to conduct the introductions? People know her, so perhaps they’d speak more freely.
Tutor: She can circulate an invitation. I wouldn’t have her sitting in the interviews. A trader might hesitate to criticise a scheme the association supported. Make clear that the project isn’t a performance review and that individual statements won’t be sent to her.
Ben: Our draft starts with, 'How has the new pedestrian area improved your business?' I can see now that assumes an improvement.
Amira: We should ask what changed, if anything, and then invite positive and negative examples. Otherwise we’d be providing the conclusion in the question.
Tutor: Good. You’ll also need to distinguish memory from records. Someone may recall fewer customers during construction, but sales figures could follow a different pattern. Ask whether records are available without making their participation depend on handing over private accounts.
Ben: We planned an audio recording rather than trying to write everything while they speak. We’d ask permission first and offer a note-only interview if they prefer.
Tutor: That’s sensible. Explain where the file will be kept and when it will be deleted. Permission to talk to you is not permission to put a voice recording on a public website.
---
Amira: We made a checklist of remaining tasks. For the recruitment invitation, we need approval from you before sending it. We’ve drafted it but haven’t contacted anyone yet.
Tutor: Send it after this meeting. The interview schedule is different: its wording needs a trial. Ask somebody outside the final sample to respond, so you can see whether the order makes sense and how long it takes.
Ben: The street map is finished. We’ve marked present businesses and former addresses from the directories, and checked that none of the symbols hides the street names.
Tutor: Fine, treat that as completed for now. Don’t spend another day improving its colours at the expense of preparation.
Amira: We also had a question asking each trader for their exact annual income. It doesn’t answer our revised question and might put people off, so we intend to remove it.
Tutor: I agree. You can ask how they describe changes without collecting an unnecessary financial profile. Remember that an interview isn’t improved merely by making it longer.
Ben: Our coding categories are still provisional. We thought we’d wait until after the first interviews before deciding the final set. We have broad interests, but people may describe problems we haven’t anticipated.
Tutor: Yes, retain that flexibility. Keep a record of changes to the categories, though. You shouldn’t quietly redefine them every time you encounter an inconvenient example.
Amira: Would it be useful to compare two of us coding the same short extract?
Tutor: Very useful, as a way to discuss interpretation rather than produce a ceremonial agreement figure. If you disagree, ask whether the categories are unclear or the account genuinely supports more than one reading. The explanation of that decision may be more valuable than an artificially neat table.

### Part 4 — The changing idea of a standard measure

Lecturer: Think about buying a length of cloth from someone you have never met. The transaction requires more than agreement about price. You need a shared understanding of length. Today I’ll discuss standard measures as a social arrangement as well as a technical achievement. A measure is useful when different people can reproduce it closely enough for the task. That does not mean every task needs the same precision. Selling cloth and testing an optical instrument place different demands on the system, even though both involve statements about distance.
Early local arrangements often used a physical reference kept in a public place. Traders could compare their own rods with it. The object represented authority as well as length: somebody had to decide who could inspect it and what happened if a trader’s measure differed. Copies made trading easier, but each copy introduced the possibility of error. Wear, damage and the method of comparison could change the result. The practical problem was not solved simply by declaring that one reference existed. People needed a dependable chain connecting everyday measures to it.
Expansion of trade increased the value of compatibility. Two towns could each have internally consistent measures and still face confusion when goods moved between them. Converting between systems required tables or specialist knowledge, and a familiar unit name did not always mean a familiar quantity. Shared measures could reduce that work, though adopting them imposed costs of its own. Tools had to be changed, labels rewritten and users trained. Resistance was not necessarily ignorance. A new system could offer broad benefits while asking particular workers to bear immediate expense.
Calibration is the process of comparing an instrument with a suitable reference and establishing how its readings relate to that reference. It does not mean making every instrument perfect. A useful calibration record can describe an error and the uncertainty around it. For example, a workshop may apply a correction when using an instrument rather than replacing it immediately. The important point is that the reading should not be treated as exact merely because it appears as a number. Extra digits on a display can create an appearance of knowledge that the measurement process does not support.
Temperature provides a straightforward example of environmental influence. A material may expand or contract as conditions change, so a physical reference needs specified conditions of use. Handling, storage and comparison procedures become part of the definition in practice. Modern systems aim for references that can be reproduced in different laboratories, but reproducibility still requires procedures and trained judgement. It is misleading to contrast an entirely social past with an entirely objective present. Social cooperation makes technical comparison possible in both cases, even when the scientific basis becomes more stable.
Documentation allows the chain of comparison to be checked. A certificate should identify the instrument, the reference, the conditions and the relevant date. Without traceability, a precise-looking result may be difficult to connect to anything beyond the local device. This matters when several organisations contribute to one product. A component made in one workshop must fit another made elsewhere, and differences may not become visible until assembly. Shared measurement practice allows organisations to investigate a mismatch rather than merely argue about whose number looks more convincing.
Finally, standards must be maintained as living arrangements. A reference that nobody can access, or a procedure that ordinary laboratories cannot follow, may fail despite theoretical elegance. Institutions provide training, comparisons and opportunities to question results. The achievement is not the elimination of every uncertainty. It is the creation of a reliable way to describe and reduce uncertainty for a particular purpose. Standard measures make cooperation possible because they organise disagreement: when numbers differ, there is a route back through evidence, conditions and references instead of only a contest of authority.

## Speaking

Part 1: 4–5 minutes. Begin with the candidate’s name and a brief introduction; no real ID document is needed for practice. Ask the following familiar-topic questions, with brief neutral follow-ups as necessary.

### Home area

- What do you like about the area where you live?

- Where do people usually meet there?

- Has the area changed since you first knew it?

### Clothes

- How do you decide what clothes to buy?

- Do you keep clothes for a long time?

- When do you need to dress more formally?

### Evenings

- What do you usually do in the evening?

- Do you prefer quiet or busy evenings?

- Were your evenings different as a child?

### Part 2 — 3–4 minutes including preparation

Describe an event you helped to organise.

- what the event was
- who you worked with
- what you were responsible for

and explain what you learned from organising it.

Give one minute for notes, then invite the candidate to speak for up to two minutes. Stop politely at two minutes. Ask one brief follow-up:

- Would you help organise it again?

- Which part was most enjoyable?

### Part 3 — 4–5 minutes

Develop the linked general discussion. Choose follow-ups that invite reasons, comparisons, examples or speculation; do not teach or correct during the interview.

- What makes a community event successful?

- Why do some people volunteer while others prefer to attend?

- Should local authorities pay for public celebrations?

- How can organisers include people with different needs?

- Do large events bring more benefits than small ones?

- How might technology change event organisation?

Assess fluency/coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range/accuracy, and pronunciation from actual speech. A transcript cannot establish pronunciation. Record only with the candidate’s consent; this pack contains no recording tool.
