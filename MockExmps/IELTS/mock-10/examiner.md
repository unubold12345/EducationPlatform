# Mock 10 — Examiner script

Keep this file away from candidates. Spoken dialogue is labelled by role. Do not speak role labels or the --- break marker. Read continuously and naturally; do not emphasise answers. The marker divides questions 1–5/6–10 within Parts 1–3; pause 30 seconds there. Part 4 has no midpoint break. Full procedure: ../ADMINISTRATION.md.

## Listening

### Part 1 — Registering for a bookshop volunteer shift

Organiser: Hello, Page Turners charity bookshop.
Caller: Hello. I saw your notice asking for volunteers. I’ve recently moved to the area and have some free time each week. I’ve worked in a shop before, though not with books. Are you still looking for people?
Organiser: We are, especially for regular shifts. We don’t expect volunteers to know every author. Being willing to ask questions and follow the stock procedure matters more. Could I take your name?
Caller: It’s Nadia Seldon. S-E-L-D-O-N. People sometimes write Sheldon, but there’s no H. I live close enough to walk, so I wouldn’t depend on the bus for an early start.
Organiser: Thank you. Which days could you offer? We have a full team on Saturday at present, but weekday afternoons are more difficult to cover.
Caller: My first thought was Wednesday, but I’ve enrolled on a Wednesday course. I can do Monday every week. I’d rather promise one reliable day than offer several and keep changing them.
Organiser: That would be excellent. The afternoon shift begins at one, but for your first visit please come at twelve thirty. The supervisor can show you the building before customers arrive for the afternoon session.
Caller: All right, 12:30 on Monday. Is it a long shift?
Organiser: Three hours normally. For the induction we’ll keep it to two if you prefer, though it’s useful to see the closing routine on a later visit. You can discuss that with the supervisor when you know your other commitments.
Caller: What kind of work needs doing? I’m happy speaking to customers, but I wouldn’t want to start on the till without training.
Organiser: You won’t. New volunteers usually begin with sorting. Donations come in mixed boxes, and we separate saleable books from items needing specialist advice. You’ll work with an experienced person rather than decide alone what an unfamiliar volume is worth.
Caller: That sounds interesting. Should I bring any documents for the registration?
Organiser: Bring one reference, please, from somebody outside your household. A former work supervisor is fine. We don’t need your school certificates or a formal essay about why you like reading.
---
Caller: Could you explain where to arrive? I know the shop front, but I imagine you don’t want me knocking while it’s closed.
Organiser: Use the rear door on your first day. It’s reached from the small lane beside the café. The front shutters will still be down at twelve thirty, and the bell there isn’t easy to hear from the stockroom.
Caller: I’ll find it. What should I wear? The notice mentioned comfortable shoes.
Organiser: Yes, and ordinary clothes you don’t mind getting dusty. We’ll provide a badge once you’ve registered. Please wear it on the shop floor so customers can tell who is working. There’s no uniform purchase.
Caller: Are volunteers expected to move heavy boxes? I can carry a few books but should avoid lifting something large from the floor.
Organiser: We have a trolley, and heavy boxes should be split into smaller loads. Tell the supervisor about any task you shouldn’t do. Nobody is expected to prove enthusiasm by taking a risk, and there’s plenty of work that doesn’t involve lifting.
Caller: Good. I assume personal belongings stay somewhere away from donations?
Organiser: There are lockers in the office. Please don’t leave a bag on the sorting table, because it could be mistaken for something brought in by a donor. Bring a small padlock if you’d like to lock your locker.
Caller: I have one at home. What if I can’t make a shift after I’ve joined?
Organiser: Call the supervisor as early as possible. Text is fine if the shop is closed, but please don’t just put a message in the social-media group. It can get buried under photographs and general conversation. A direct message lets us arrange cover.
Caller: Understood. I’ll get the reference organised and see you on Monday.
Organiser: I’ll email the induction outline today. Read it before you come if you can, but don’t worry about memorising the stock categories. The first visit is for learning the system, not passing a test.

### Part 2 — Tour of the Westfield recreation centre

Guide: Welcome to Westfield. I’ll explain the rooms using the plan on your information sheet, then mention changes to our membership arrangements. You are standing at reception, shown at the bottom of the plan. The corridor runs straight towards the outdoor court at the top. Left and right refer to the direction you’re facing now, not to somebody returning from the court. The central corridor stays clear; equipment should never be left there, even for a minute between classes.
The room immediately to your right is the equipment store. It has double doors, so returning larger items is easier. Members can borrow rackets there, but staff need to record the number before you take them outside. Please don’t help yourself just because another customer has left the door open. Opposite, immediately to your left, is a staff room that is not open to members.
Walk towards the court and you’ll reach two more rooms. The first on the left is the exercise studio. This is where stretching and low-impact classes take place. There are mirrors along one wall, but don’t confuse it with the dance studio at our other building. Across the corridor, the first room on the right beyond the store is the changing area. There are accessible facilities within it, so you don’t need to ask for a separate key at reception.
Continue to the last pair of rooms. On the left, just before the door to the court, is the first-aid room. It is staffed during all organised sessions. On the right is the meeting room, where club organisers can discuss events without standing in reception. That room used to contain vending machines. We’ve moved refreshments into the entrance area to free it for meetings and small training sessions.
---
Outdoor-court bookings are now released seven days ahead. Previously, some members reserved several weeks in advance and then cancelled shortly before playing, leaving little opportunity for others. The shorter window is intended to improve access. It isn’t a response to resurfacing work; that was finished last month and doesn’t affect the current schedule.
Equipment hire is included in the membership price for ordinary sessions. However, lost items must still be paid for. We don’t want members to avoid trying a new sport because they haven’t bought the right racket, but borrowing carries responsibility. If you find a defect before playing, show staff so that it isn’t attributed to you when you return the item.
Our introductory classes have a new attendance rule. You may reserve a place without paying an extra class fee, but if you cannot come, cancel by the previous evening. Two unexplained absences in a month lead to a temporary booking restriction. The aim is to release unused places, not collect penalties from people whose circumstances change unexpectedly.
Children under twelve must be accompanied in the building, except when handed over to a registered children’s class. An adult waiting in the café has not automatically completed that handover. Please speak to the class leader, who keeps a register. If you are collecting for another family, make sure the arrangement has been recorded before the session ends.
Finally, we are looking for feedback on quieter opening periods. We already know that Saturday morning is busy. What we need now is information from people who would use the centre at other times if a particular activity were offered. The short form asks about feasible times as well as preferred activities. A popular suggestion isn’t useful if everyone who chooses it is unavailable when the room can actually be staffed.

### Part 3 — Testing a model bridge

Tutor: Your bridge project report is due next week. How did the first test go?
Nora: The model carried more weight than we expected, but it failed suddenly. We thought the main beam would bend first. Instead, a connection near the support pulled apart.
Ethan: We used the right dimensions from the drawing, so at first we blamed the material. Looking again at the video, we saw the connection moving before the beam changed shape.
Tutor: That’s an important observation. A structure is not stronger than every point through which the load must pass. Did you record where each weight was placed?
Nora: Yes, but our first diagram wasn’t precise enough. The weights were spread over a wider area in one test. We need a loading template so that the comparison isn’t affected by where we happen to set them down.
Ethan: We also want to repeat the test on more than one model. Otherwise one badly made connection could be mistaken for the inevitable behaviour of the design.
Tutor: Sensible. Keep construction records so you can discuss differences. Don’t quietly discard a model because it performs less well than you hoped. A poor result may be telling you something about how difficult the design is to build consistently.
Nora: We considered changing the material and the joint together, but then we’d be unable to tell which change mattered.
Ethan: So we’ll first change only the joint, keeping the other dimensions and material the same. If that improves performance, we’ll still need to say how many tests support the claim.
Tutor: Good. What will you count as failure? Total collapse is easy to identify, but a real bridge can become unusable before that.
Nora: We can measure deflection at the centre and set a limit before testing. The report will distinguish exceeding that limit from final collapse.
Tutor: Exactly. A stated criterion makes the comparison meaningful. It also prevents you from redefining success after seeing which version looks best.
---
Ethan: For the next test, we need some equipment. The scale is available in our lab, but the displacement sensor requires booking from the engineering store. We should reserve that today rather than assume it will be there.
Nora: I can arrange the sensor. What about the loading template?
Ethan: That’s the piece we should try out before the main test. The current cardboard version slides when we add weights. If it changes position, the careful markings won’t help us.
Tutor: Make a pilot version and check the practical handling. A procedure can look rigorous on paper and still be impossible to carry out consistently with the hands and space available.
Nora: The safety screen has already been checked by the technician. We have written confirmation, so that item is complete.
Tutor: Keep the confirmation with the risk assessment. Nobody should stand in line with a joint that might release a fragment.
Ethan: Our draft introduction includes a long history of famous bridges. It takes up most of the word allowance without explaining our experiment. We’ll remove that section and use the space for the joint design and testing procedure.
Tutor: A brief context is enough. The reader needs to understand your question, not prove that you have looked at many photographs.
Nora: Finally, the cost comparison. We can’t finish it until we know how much extra material the revised joint uses. We’ll calculate that after constructing the final models.
Ethan: And include labour time separately. A joint that uses very little material but takes much longer to make might not be the better option in every setting.
Tutor: Excellent. Just keep the scope clear. A classroom model can demonstrate a principle and expose construction issues. It cannot certify the safety of a full-size bridge. Your conclusions should be ambitious enough to answer your question and restrained enough to respect that difference.

### Part 4 — Designing a household waste audit

Lecturer: A town trying to reduce household waste needs to know more than the weight arriving at a disposal site. It needs an account of what is discarded, where it comes from and which changes could realistically prevent it. Today we’ll follow the design of a fictional waste audit. The first step is to define the boundary. Does the study cover homes only, or also restaurants and offices? If these sources are mixed without a record, a large quantity of food waste might be wrongly attributed to households when businesses produced much of it.
The team then defines the period. A holiday week may differ from an ordinary working week, and one season may not represent the year. Repeating observations can reveal variation, but the budget rarely permits continuous measurement everywhere. Researchers therefore choose sampling dates deliberately and state what they cannot represent. They should avoid selecting only the easiest collection days. A timetable is part of the method, not an administrative detail that can be changed casually once inconvenient conditions appear.
Recruitment matters too. Volunteers who are especially interested in reducing waste may already behave differently from other households. A study based entirely on those volunteers could underestimate ordinary discarding. The fictional team selects addresses from a defined list and makes several attempts to invite each selected household. Participation remains optional, and non-response is recorded. Researchers cannot compel people to share private details. They can, however, describe how the eventual sample differs from the population they hoped to study rather than silently calling it representative.
Before weighing begins, the team establishes categories. Too few categories hide useful distinctions; too many make sorting slow and unreliable. Food that might have been eaten is separated from unavoidable remains within the study’s stated definition. Packaging is classified by material where this can be done consistently. Staff practise on a pilot sample and discuss ambiguous items. A written guide records decisions so that two sorters do not place the same object in different categories simply because each has a different intuition about where it belongs.
Containers have weight of their own. Scales are checked, and the weight of an empty container is recorded so it can be subtracted from the total. Otherwise a heavy collection box could look like a household producing more rubbish. Wet conditions create another complication: absorbed water can alter measured mass. The team records relevant conditions and uses a consistent handling procedure. Apparent numerical precision is not a substitute for knowing what entered the weighing process. A balance showing many decimal places cannot correct a badly defined measurement.
Privacy requires practical arrangements throughout. Bags may contain names, addresses or information about household routines. Staff receive training, identifiable material is not photographed for publicity, and records use codes rather than residents' names. The public report describes groups, not a league table of individual homes. The purpose is to improve a service and identify preventable waste, not embarrass participants into a particular performance. If people fear exposure, they may change what they put out or decline to take part, damaging both trust and the evidence.
Analysis compares like with like where possible. Total weight alone can be misleading when household sizes differ, so the team records the number of residents and considers an appropriate rate. Yet one rate cannot explain every difference: a household may have visitors, work away or use another disposal route. Interviews can add context but also introduce recall errors. The report keeps direct measurements distinct from explanations offered afterwards. This allows readers to see which conclusions rest on the weighing and which remain hypotheses about behaviour.
Finally, an audit should connect its findings to actions that can be checked. If a proposed collection change is introduced, follow-up measurement can ask whether the targeted material actually declines and whether it moves into another stream. A fall in one bin is not automatically a reduction in total waste. The achievement is an evidence-based cycle: define, sample, measure, interpret, act and reassess. Each step makes the next more useful when its assumptions are visible, and each can mislead when a convenient number is treated as the whole story.

## Speaking

Part 1: 4–5 minutes. Begin with the candidate’s name and a brief introduction; no real ID document is needed for practice. Ask the following familiar-topic questions, with brief neutral follow-ups as necessary.

### Daily travel

- How do you usually make short journeys?

- Is there a journey you particularly enjoy?

- Would you like to travel differently in future?

### Writing

- When do you write things by hand?

- Do you keep notes for yourself?

- Has the amount you write changed?

### Free time

- Do you prefer planning your free time?

- What do you do when you have only a short break?

- Who do you usually spend free time with?

### Part 2 — 3–4 minutes including preparation

Describe a decision you made that took some time.

- what you had to decide
- what options you considered
- who or what helped you

and explain why you took time to make the decision.

Give one minute for notes, then invite the candidate to speak for up to two minutes. Stop politely at two minutes. Ask one brief follow-up:

- Were you satisfied with the result?

- Would the decision be easier now?

### Part 3 — 4–5 minutes

Develop the linked general discussion. Choose follow-ups that invite reasons, comparisons, examples or speculation; do not teach or correct during the interview.

- Which decisions should young people make for themselves?

- When is it useful to consult several people?

- Why can having many options make a decision difficult?

- Should organisations explain difficult decisions publicly?

- How might time pressure affect decision quality?

- Can a good decision still have a disappointing result?

Assess fluency/coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range/accuracy, and pronunciation from actual speech. A transcript cannot establish pronunciation. Record only with the candidate’s consent; this pack contains no recording tool.
