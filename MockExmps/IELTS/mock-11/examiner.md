# Mock 11 — Examiner script

Keep this file away from candidates. Spoken dialogue is labelled by role. Do not speak role labels or the --- break marker. Read continuously and naturally; do not emphasise answers. The marker divides questions 1–5/6–10 within Parts 1–3; pause 30 seconds there. Part 4 has no midpoint break. Full procedure: ../ADMINISTRATION.md.

## Listening

### Part 1 — Booking a short residential course

Administrator: Oak House adult learning centre. How may I help?
Caller: I’d like information about the weekend course on making radio programmes. I’ve listened to the sample online and it sounds practical, but I need to check accommodation and travel before booking. I’ll be coming from another town.
Administrator: Certainly. The course includes a room on Friday and Saturday nights, though meals have separate arrangements. Could I take your surname in case we need to continue the booking later?
Caller: It’s Varden, V-A-R-D-E-N. I’ve never stayed at the centre. Is the building close to public transport, or would I need to hire a car?
Administrator: You won’t need a car. Come to Brook station, not the larger station at Hillgate. People sometimes choose Hillgate because more trains stop there, but the onward connection is much less convenient. From Brook there’s a local bus that stops at our gate.
Caller: I can get there on Friday evening. What time can I collect the room key? The train I’d prefer arrives at about five.
Administrator: Reception opens for arriving residents at four thirty, so 4:30 is the earliest. Before then the rooms are being checked. If your train is later, someone will be here until eight. Please tell us if you expect to arrive after that rather than assuming the front door will stay open.
Caller: Fine. What is the full course fee? I saw two prices on an old leaflet.
Administrator: The residential weekend is one hundred and sixty-eight pounds. The lower figure, one hundred and twenty, is for people who attend teaching sessions but sleep elsewhere. It isn’t a discount available just because you’re travelling by train. Your booking would be £168 before optional meals.
Caller: And how much do I pay to secure a place?
Administrator: A deposit of forty pounds. The rest is due two weeks before the course. We can hold a provisional place until tomorrow while you check the trains, but it isn’t confirmed until we receive the deposit.
---
Caller: Are the rooms shared? I’m happy working with other people all day, but I’d prefer somewhere quiet in the evening.
Administrator: Most are single rooms. There are a few twins for friends booking together, but we never assign a stranger to share without agreement. You’ll have a single room. Towels are included; bring your own toiletries.
Caller: What about meals? I don’t eat meat, and sometimes a residential course provides only a sandwich if you mention it too late.
Administrator: We can provide vegetarian meals. Please put vegetarian on the booking form rather than only telling the tutor, because the kitchen uses the form to plan. Breakfast is included in the room price. You can buy lunch and dinner here or use places in the village.
Caller: Do participants need to bring recording equipment? I have a laptop, but no special microphone.
Administrator: We’ll provide microphones and recorders. Bring headphones if you have a comfortable pair. We have some spares, but people often prefer their own for editing. Your laptop can be useful, though it’s not a requirement and we can supply a shared computer.
Caller: I may have a large bag because I’m continuing to visit family afterwards. Is there somewhere to leave it on Sunday after checkout?
Administrator: Yes, bags can stay in the office until the last session ends. They shouldn’t be left in the corridor, which is an emergency route. The workshop itself is on the ground floor, so you won’t need to carry the bag upstairs and down again.
Caller: Good. Is there preparation to complete before arriving?
Administrator: Please bring a photograph of a place you know. You’ll use it as a starting point for a short spoken piece. It needn’t be artistic, and it won’t be assessed as a photograph. We’re interested in the story it helps you tell, not the camera you used.
Caller: That sounds manageable. I’ll check the train now and complete the form tonight.
Administrator: I’ll send the travel sheet and provisional booking reference. Check the dates before paying, as there is a second radio course in the following month and the accommodation arrangements differ slightly.

### Part 2 — A guide to the town’s open-building weekend

Guide: Welcome to the information session for our open-building weekend. Several places normally closed to the public will offer visits, and the arrangements differ between sites. Please don’t assume that the ticket for one building lets you enter all the others. Most visits are free, but we still need to manage numbers and protect working spaces. I’ll explain five buildings first, then some general advice for planning your day. The leaflet contains addresses, and the updated website has the latest cancellation information.
The old courthouse is popular because people want to see the hearing room and the cells below. Only a limited number can enter each tour, so you must reserve a timed place before the weekend. We won’t maintain a queue for spare places at the door. If you can’t attend, cancel so someone else can use it. Booking several times in the hope of deciding later makes this much harder.
The clock workshop is reached by a steep staircase. There is no lift, and the narrow turn means we cannot install a temporary ramp. The ground-floor display remains available to everyone, but a visit to the workshop itself involves stairs. The leaflet states this clearly because we don’t want someone travelling across town only to discover the restriction on arrival.
At the recording studio, visitors can try making a short sound effect under supervision. Bring nothing special; the materials are supplied. The session is designed around participation rather than standing silently while someone describes equipment. Your recording can be sent to you afterwards if you choose to provide an address, but you don’t have to give one to take part.
The water tower offers the best view, but its opening depends on conditions. Strong wind makes the exposed upper platform unsuitable for visitors. Check the morning update before leaving home. We don’t cancel just because it’s cloudy, but safety takes priority over keeping a promise printed weeks earlier. A closure there doesn’t mean every other building will be closed.
The municipal greenhouse will stay open later than the other sites, until eight in the evening. That may help people who work during the day. Please use the marked paths because staff will still be maintaining plants in some sections. You can see those activities, but the site isn’t being converted into an unrestricted evening park.
---
For travel, we recommend the temporary circular bus. A ticket covers repeated journeys during the day, and the bus stops near all five sites. Walking between two nearby buildings is easy, but the whole circuit is longer than it appears on the small leaflet map. The service is intended to make combinations possible, not to require visitors to see every location in one day.
Please arrive for a reserved tour about ten minutes early. Earlier than that, there may be nowhere suitable to wait. A guide cannot delay the start for late arrivals because the next group needs its own full visit. If you miss a start, contact the booking desk rather than attempting to join through a side entrance.
Photography rules vary. You may photograph public displays where signs permit, but working documents on desks are not part of the exhibition. Ask before taking an identifiable photograph of an employee. Some are doing their ordinary jobs while visits take place, and being visible to the public isn’t agreement to appear in somebody’s online album.
Children are welcome on the family routes shown in the leaflet. Other tours include lengthy explanations or restricted spaces, so please read the age guidance. It isn’t a judgement about whether a particular child is well behaved. It’s information about what the organiser can provide safely and comfortably within that building.
At the end of a visit, you may be invited to suggest questions for next year’s programme. We particularly want to know what you’d like to understand about the work done inside these buildings. Requests simply to open more places are welcome, but a specific question helps us plan a useful visit rather than merely unlock another door. Staff have volunteered their time, and good questions make the exchange rewarding for them too.

### Part 3 — Preparing a student radio feature

Tutor: You’ve brought a draft plan for your radio feature. What’s the focus now?
Lena: We started with a general history of the night bus. It sounded too much like a list of dates, so we’re focusing on the people who use the last service and what the journey means in their routines.
Omar: We’d like to include different voices without pretending that three interviews represent everyone who travels at night.
Tutor: Good. What sound will help listeners understand the setting?
Lena: We recorded the bus arriving, but the engine overwhelms most of the conversation at the stop. We shouldn’t just turn up the voices because that also makes the background louder.
Omar: We could use a short arrival sound separately, then conduct the interviews a little way from the road. The sound can establish the scene without sitting under every spoken sentence.
Tutor: That would improve clarity. Remember that an atmospheric recording is useful only if it serves the story. You don’t need continuous noise to prove you were there.
Lena: We interviewed one passenger already. He gave a lovely account, but asked not to be identifiable. Could we keep the voice and omit the name?
Tutor: A distinctive voice or workplace detail may still identify him. Discuss the intended use with him and offer an alternative, such as reading an agreed extract with another speaker. Don’t assume that removing a name solves every privacy issue.
Omar: The transport office offered an official spokesperson. We should include that view, but perhaps not let it be the only explanation of why the timetable changed.
Tutor: Exactly. Ask which records support the explanation, and compare it with other evidence. Official status is relevant to the speaker’s role, not a guarantee that every interpretation is complete.
Lena: Our biggest editing problem is that all the interviews are interesting. We have enough for twenty minutes but the assignment allows eight.
Omar: We need to return to the central question and select material that advances it, even if a favourite anecdote has to go.
Tutor: That’s the discipline. Cutting should be guided by purpose, not by whether a sentence is easy to remove without changing your preferred argument.
---
Lena: We’ve divided the remaining work into five items. The opening sequence is drafted, but we want to play it to someone who hasn’t heard the story. If they can’t identify the setting and question, we should revise it.
Omar: The permission for the passenger extract is still unresolved. We’ll contact him again before using anything, not treat the earlier interview as blanket consent.
Tutor: Good. Put that ahead of polishing transitions. A beautifully edited extract you can’t use isn’t progress towards a finished feature.
Lena: The transport timetable is checked. We compared the current version with the archived one and confirmed the change date with the office, so that part is complete.
Omar: Our long music introduction is another matter. It takes almost a minute, which is too much in an eight-minute programme. We’ll remove it and retain only a brief transition where it helps listeners follow the change of scene.
Tutor: Ensure you have rights to whatever music remains. There is no need for a grand opening merely because professional programmes sometimes use one.
Lena: The final title is undecided. We have a working title, but we’ll choose the public one after the edit, when we know what the piece actually emphasises.
Omar: Otherwise we might force the material to fulfil a clever title we invented before listening properly. We’ll keep a few alternatives and ask what each one promises the audience.
Tutor: Sensible. When you deliver, include a short production note describing major editorial choices. Listeners won’t hear every decision, but the note should explain why you selected voices, separated sound from speech and omitted material. It makes the learning visible without turning the feature itself into a lecture about editing.

### Part 4 — Place names as historical evidence

Lecturer: A place name can remain familiar long after the feature it once described has disappeared. A street called Mill Lane may contain no mill, and a district named after a field may now be covered with houses. Today we’ll consider how names can contribute to historical enquiry and why they should not be treated as complete records by themselves. Our examples come from an invented district, so the purpose is to explain a method rather than make claims about a particular real community’s language or origins.
The first step is to collect forms of the name from different periods. Modern spelling may conceal earlier variations, and a single old map may contain an error. Researchers compare maps with deeds, directories and other dated documents. The sequence does not always show a neat progression from one form to another. Two spellings may coexist for many years. A record should preserve that variation instead of silently converting every historical form into the version printed on today’s sign, because the difference itself may be relevant evidence.
Pronunciation adds another source. Residents may say a name in a way that differs from what an outsider expects from its spelling. Interviews can preserve these spoken forms, though no individual speaker automatically represents an entire district. Age, neighbourhood and family history may all matter. With permission, researchers record speech and note the context of the interview. A recording without information about who was speaking and when can become difficult to interpret later, even when the sound quality is excellent.
Meaning is particularly tempting. A familiar modern word may appear inside a name, inviting an easy story about its origin. Yet the resemblance could be accidental, or the form could have changed from a different earlier word. Researchers need historical and linguistic evidence before accepting the attractive explanation. Local stories remain valuable as accounts of how residents understand a place, but that is not identical to demonstrating how the name first arose. The two questions can be studied together without pretending they have the same answer.
Maps also reveal spatial uncertainty. A name may move from a small feature to a larger area, or survive after a road is redirected. The modern boundary of a neighbourhood should not automatically be projected backwards onto every old reference. Researchers record what geographical extent a document appears to mean. Sometimes a description of adjoining properties is more useful than a bold label at the centre of a map. Names belong to practices of locating and describing places, not merely to fixed points waiting unchanged through time.
Official renaming creates another layer. A council decision may introduce a new name on signs while residents continue using the old one. Administrative records establish the formal change date, but everyday adoption can be gradual and uneven. Delivery workers, schools and local newspapers may use different forms. Studying that transition requires several kinds of evidence. A decree tells us what was authorised, not exactly what everyone said the following morning. Treating official documents as a complete account of usage would miss a large part of the social history.
The project must also consider consent and sensitivity. Some names carry associations that residents find painful, while others are closely connected with a minority community’s identity. Publishing an origin story can affect present debates, even when the researcher thinks of the work as purely historical. This is not a reason to suppress evidence, but it is a reason to explain uncertainty and consult people who may be affected. A neat public label should not turn a disputed interpretation into an apparently settled fact just because a display has limited space.
Finally, a useful catalogue links each claim to its source. It separates an attested form from a proposed meaning and records alternatives where the evidence remains inconclusive. The aim is not to make every name mysterious. Some have clear and well-supported histories. It is to prevent the ease of repeating a story from being mistaken for the strength of its evidence. Names can connect everyday journeys to a deeper history when researchers preserve the route by which they reached their conclusions, including the points where that route still divides.

## Speaking

Part 1: 4–5 minutes. Begin with the candidate’s name and a brief introduction; no real ID document is needed for practice. Ask the following familiar-topic questions, with brief neutral follow-ups as necessary.

### Home

- Which part of your home do you use most?

- What makes a home comfortable for you?

- Would you like to move in future?

### Music

- When do you listen to music?

- Do people in your family enjoy similar music?

- Have you ever tried learning an instrument?

### Shopping

- Do you usually make a shopping list?

- Do you prefer small shops or large ones?

- What do you avoid buying online?

### Part 2 — 3–4 minutes including preparation

Describe a local change that you welcomed.

- what changed
- when you first noticed it
- who benefited from it

and explain why you were pleased about the change.

Give one minute for notes, then invite the candidate to speak for up to two minutes. Stop politely at two minutes. Ask one brief follow-up:

- Did you discuss it with other people?

- Would you like another similar change?

### Part 3 — 4–5 minutes

Develop the linked general discussion. Choose follow-ups that invite reasons, comparisons, examples or speculation; do not teach or correct during the interview.

- How should towns decide which improvements to make first?

- Why do people disagree about changes to familiar places?

- Should residents always be consulted before public changes?

- How can a town keep its character while meeting new needs?

- Who should pay to maintain shared spaces?

- How can authorities tell whether an improvement has worked?

Assess fluency/coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range/accuracy, and pronunciation from actual speech. A transcript cannot establish pronunciation. Record only with the candidate’s consent; this pack contains no recording tool.
