# Mock 12 — Examiner script

Keep this file away from candidates. Spoken dialogue is labelled by role. Do not speak role labels or the --- break marker. Read continuously and naturally; do not emphasise answers. The marker divides questions 1–5/6–10 within Parts 1–3; pause 30 seconds there. Part 4 has no midpoint break. Full procedure: ../ADMINISTRATION.md.

## Listening

### Part 1 — Hiring equipment for a local history event

Assistant: Community Equipment Hire, this is Nina.
Caller: Hello. We’re holding a small local history evening and need some equipment. The hall has chairs and tables but no way to show photographs clearly to a group. I’ve never hired from you, so could you explain the process?
Assistant: Of course. We lend to community groups as well as individuals. Could I take your surname and the date first? Availability depends on the day, especially during the festival period.
Caller: It’s Harker, H-A-R-K-E-R. The event is on the nineteenth of October. We originally planned the eighteenth, but the hall was booked, so please use 19 October.
Assistant: I have that. What would you like to hire? We have a large television display or a projector with a separate screen. The display is easier in daylight but more difficult to transport.
Caller: We’ll close the curtains, so a projector should be fine. We don’t need a microphone; there will only be twenty people and the room is small. A projector is the main thing, plus the screen if that’s separate.
Assistant: It is separate, but included in the same community rate. Collection can be on the event day or the afternoon before. If you haven’t used the equipment, I’d recommend the previous day so you can test your files in the hall.
Caller: The previous afternoon works. I finish work at three, so could I collect at four?
Assistant: Yes, 4:00 is fine. Please allow twenty minutes for us to demonstrate the connections. We can’t leave the equipment outside for a quick pickup if you arrive after we close.
Caller: What will the charge be?
Assistant: Twenty-eight pounds for the projector and screen together. The forty-pound rate on one part of the website is for a commercial booking. We’ll also take a refundable security deposit, but the actual hire charge for your group is £28. Bring the booking confirmation so the desk applies the correct category.
---
Caller: My laptop is fairly new. Do I need an extra cable?
Assistant: Bring your laptop for the demonstration and we can check its sockets. We supply the ordinary connection cable, and an adapter if required. Please don’t buy an adapter before we know which one you need; they can look similar but serve different functions.
Caller: Good. What identification do you require? I don’t have a driving licence.
Assistant: A passport is fine. We also need proof of the address used for the booking, but that can be a recent bill. We don’t keep a copy of the passport image, just a record that identification was checked.
Caller: Will the equipment fit in a small car?
Assistant: The projector comes in a padded case. The folded screen is longer and might need the rear seats down. Check the dimensions in the confirmation before travelling. Two people aren’t normally necessary, but don’t force the screen into a space that bends its frame.
Caller: We’ll test it as you suggest. The photographs are in a presentation. Should I rely on the hall’s internet connection for the final few images?
Assistant: I’d save a backup locally, ideally on a memory stick as well as on the laptop. The projector doesn’t supply internet access, and a connection problem shouldn’t stop the whole evening. Check that the files open without needing an online account.
Caller: When do we return everything? The event ends late, so the next morning would be easiest.
Assistant: Return by ten the next morning. Bring it to the warehouse entrance, which is around the back; collections use the front desk. Staff at the warehouse check all the pieces before releasing the security deposit. If an item is missing, call us rather than making another journey with an incomplete set and hoping nobody notices.
Caller: Thank you. I’ll make the booking online and bring the laptop when I collect it.
Assistant: Look for the community-group category, and I’ll attach a note confirming the equipment we’ve discussed.

### Part 2 — An induction for citizen weather observers

Coordinator: Welcome to the weather-observation project. We are comparing conditions across different parts of the town, and your observations will complement the fixed station at the college. They won’t replace it. A single official site gives a consistent long-term record, while a local network can reveal variation within the town. Please remember that a higher number of observations isn’t useful if the method changes unpredictably from one person to another. Today’s induction explains the details that need to remain consistent.
First, equipment remains the property of the project. It is lent without charge, but please report a fault promptly instead of making an unrecorded replacement. Two instruments sold under the same broad description may behave differently. We can compare a replacement before it goes into use and mark the date of the change. Otherwise a difference caused by equipment might look like a sudden change in your neighbourhood’s weather.
Site selection comes next. Choose somewhere you can reach safely and repeatedly, but don’t assume the easiest windowsill is suitable. Heat from a wall or an outlet can affect a reading. The project officer will visit to agree a position with you. If you later need to move an instrument, contact us first. A temporary building or growing hedge may matter even if the new spot is only a short distance away.
Please make the standard observation at the agreed time, not whenever you happen to remember. If you miss it, record the omission. Don’t enter a plausible-looking number from a phone forecast to fill the gap. A visible missing value is more useful than a hidden mixture of observed and predicted data. You can add a note explaining the problem, which helps us distinguish a forgotten reading from equipment failure.
For severe conditions, your safety comes before a reading. Stay indoors if going out would be dangerous. Nobody should climb over a fallen branch to preserve a perfect attendance record. The analysis will account for missing observations, and the project is not a competition to collect data at any cost.
---
There are five routine records to keep, each with a particular instruction. For the thermometer reading, wait until the instrument has settled after you approach. Don’t hold it in your hand while reading, because your body can influence the result. The project guide explains the short waiting procedure, and it is the same for everyone.
For rainfall, empty the collector after taking the daily reading. Otherwise yesterday’s water can be counted again. If you’ve been away, note the longer collection period rather than dividing the total into invented daily values. We need to know what interval the measurement actually covers.
For cloud observations, use the reference sheet. Its examples help distinguish the categories used in this project. You don’t need to memorise a professional atlas. The aim is consistency in a limited set of observations, not to claim that every complicated sky can be described perfectly by one simple label.
For wind direction, report the direction the wind comes from. This is easy to reverse if you focus on where a flag is pointing away from its support. Practise with the officer during the first visit, and ask if the local buildings make the indication unclear.
Finally, for unusual events, add a photograph where it is safe and practical. A picture of ice or a flooded path can help interpret a short written note. Don’t photograph identifiable neighbours without permission, and don’t enter private land to improve a view. The photo is supporting evidence, not a reason to take a risk.
You will receive a monthly summary showing how your record fits the network. Differences aren’t automatically errors. Part of the purpose is to understand real local variation, while recording enough context to distinguish it from the effects of instruments and methods.

### Part 3 — Comparing two audio guides

Tutor: I understand you’re comparing audio guides at the gallery. What is the question?
Alice: Whether a guide told as a short story changes what visitors remember compared with a factual list. We don’t want to assume that a story is necessarily better, just because we find it more entertaining.
Jamal: Both versions will cover the same three paintings. We need to keep the information content comparable, though the organisation will differ.
Tutor: And length? A longer guide could produce more recall simply because it includes more exposure.
Alice: We’ve edited both to about four minutes, read by the same speaker at the same pace. We first used two voices, but that would introduce another difference, so we’ve removed it.
Jamal: We also need to avoid people hearing both versions and recognising repeated facts. We plan separate visitor groups rather than having everyone listen twice.
Tutor: How will you assign them?
Alice: We’ll use a pre-set random list, not let people choose story or list. Otherwise people with different interests might select the version they expect to like, and the groups would already differ.
Jamal: The invitation will say it’s a study of guide formats, without calling one the creative version. We shouldn’t encourage a preference before anyone has heard it.
Tutor: Good. How will you assess memory?
Alice: With a short set of questions after the visit. Some ask for facts from the guide, and one asks visitors to connect a painting with an explanation. We’re not measuring everything they learn in the gallery.
Jamal: We should ask about prior familiarity too. Someone who knew a painting already might answer correctly without relying on either guide.
Tutor: Yes. Record that and explain how you use it in analysis. Be careful not to discard knowledgeable visitors after seeing which condition they were in. Your inclusion rules should be defined before collecting results.
---
Alice: For the practical tasks, the audio files are edited but need a listening trial on the actual devices. They sound clear on our laptops, but we haven’t checked the gallery headsets.
Jamal: We can pilot those tomorrow. The memory questions need tutor approval. We’ve drafted them, but we’d like you to check that none gives away an answer to another.
Tutor: Send them today. I can review the content, but the pilot should also test how long the questionnaire takes. A theoretically good question can still be confusing to a tired visitor standing near the exit.
Alice: The gallery permission is complete. The education officer sent the signed approval this morning, including the times when we can approach visitors.
Jamal: We originally included a question asking visitors to rate the café. It’s unrelated and comes from a template we borrowed, so we’re going to delete it rather than collect information with no clear use.
Tutor: Good. A template is a starting point, not authority to ask every question printed on it.
Alice: The final comparison chart will wait until we have data. We know the variables, but if scores cluster near the maximum we may need a display that shows that clearly, not just two broad average bars.
Jamal: We’ll decide the presentation after looking at the distribution, and explain the choice. We won’t change the research question to make the chart more exciting.
Tutor: That’s the right distinction. Analysis needs a plan, while presentation should remain responsive to the data’s shape. Keep records of decisions so a reader can follow the route from question to conclusion.
Alice: The gallery hopes the story version will work well because it is considering a larger production. We’ll make clear that our small study can inform that decision but cannot guarantee the same effect for every painting or visitor.
Tutor: Exactly. Useful evidence is not the same as a universal promise, and an honest limitation need not make the project unhelpful.

### Part 4 — Why museum stores need good information

Lecturer: A museum store is often imagined as a room full of objects waiting to appear in an exhibition. In practice, it is an information system with physical responsibilities. An object that is preserved perfectly but cannot be found is unavailable for research. An object that can be found but has lost its identity may be equally difficult to interpret. Today we’ll follow the basic journey of an item entering a fictional local museum, concentrating on the connection between its physical location and its documentary record.
The museum first establishes ownership. A donation, a loan and an item left temporarily for identification create different obligations. Staff should not assume that possession means permission to display, alter or dispose of an object. The agreement records what has been transferred and which conditions apply. This may sound administrative, but uncertainty here can undermine later decisions, especially when the person who brought the item is no longer available to explain the original conversation. A clear record protects the museum and the people connected with the object.
Each accepted object receives a unique number. That number links the item to its catalogue record, photographs and associated documents. It should not depend on a shelf location, because objects move. A label that says only where an item currently sits becomes misleading when a store is rearranged. Staff use an appropriate marking method that does not damage the material, and detachable parts need a documented relationship to the whole. Otherwise a lid and a container can become two apparently unrelated mysteries after being separated for treatment.
A condition report describes the object’s state at entry. It distinguishes existing damage from changes observed later. Good photographs help, but a photograph alone may not show a loose joint, an unstable surface or a part hidden from view. Staff combine images with written observations and specify which areas could not be inspected. The report should not make an uncertain diagnosis sound definitive. A mark that might be mould requires investigation and precaution, not a confident label added by someone without the relevant expertise.
Storage supports are chosen for the item, rather than forcing every object into identical packaging. A heavy piece may need support across its base; a fragile projection may need space around it. The support should allow safe handling without unnecessary direct contact. Where practical, a tray can move with the object. Shelving must also accommodate retrieval, since packing objects densely can make access hazardous. The apparent efficiency of filling every gap disappears if retrieving one item requires moving several vulnerable neighbours.
Location records must change whenever an object moves. This includes temporary moves to photography, conservation or an educational session, not just permanent transfers between stores. The record identifies who moved it, when and where it is expected to be. A temporary location left undocumented can become a long-term disappearance. Staff therefore check movements as part of ordinary work rather than waiting for an annual inventory to reveal that something is missing. Regular audits test whether the system describes what is actually on the shelves.
The catalogue also contains interpretation: what an object may have been used for, who made it and which evidence supports those claims. New research can revise these accounts. The system should retain a history of changes instead of silently replacing an earlier attribution. A researcher may need to understand why an object was once described differently. Separating observation from interpretation helps too. Dimensions measured directly have a different status from a maker’s name inferred from a resemblance, even when both appear in the same database entry.
Finally, digital records need their own care. Backups should be tested, and access permissions should protect sensitive information without making routine work impossible. A copy that has never been restored offers uncertain protection. The museum also needs a plan for future software changes so that records remain usable when a particular application becomes obsolete. Caring for a collection means caring for the connections that make objects meaningful. The physical store and the catalogue are partners: neither can fulfil the museum’s responsibility while the other is neglected.

## Speaking

Part 1: 4–5 minutes. Begin with the candidate’s name and a brief introduction; no real ID document is needed for practice. Ask the following familiar-topic questions, with brief neutral follow-ups as necessary.

### Work or study

- Why did you choose your current work or course?

- What skill would make it easier?

- Do you prefer working alone or with others?

### Colours

- What colours do you enjoy having around you?

- Do you choose colours carefully when buying things?

- Did you like different colours as a child?

### Timekeeping

- How do you keep track of appointments?

- Are you usually early or just on time?

- What do you do when somebody is late?

### Part 2 — 3–4 minutes including preparation

Describe a person who explained something clearly to you.

- who the person was
- what they explained
- how they made it clear

and explain why their explanation helped you.

Give one minute for notes, then invite the candidate to speak for up to two minutes. Stop politely at two minutes. Ask one brief follow-up:

- Did you ask any questions?

- Have you used what you learned since then?

### Part 3 — 4–5 minutes

Develop the linked general discussion. Choose follow-ups that invite reasons, comparisons, examples or speculation; do not teach or correct during the interview.

- What makes an explanation easy to understand?

- How should explanations differ for children and adults?

- Are pictures always better than words for teaching?

- Why do experts sometimes find it difficult to explain simple things?

- Should public services explain their decisions in plain language?

- Can a simple explanation become misleading?

Assess fluency/coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range/accuracy, and pronunciation from actual speech. A transcript cannot establish pronunciation. Record only with the candidate’s consent; this pack contains no recording tool.
