> **City Noise and Birdsong**
>
> In many cities, traffic and machinery produce a constant, low-pitched roar. For birds, which rely on song to attract mates and defend their territory, this noise is a serious obstacle. A song that cannot be heard is of little use to the singer.
>
> Researchers have found that several species adjust their behavior in response. Great tits living in noisy urban areas, for example, sing at a higher pitch than those in nearby forests, which allows their songs to rise above the rumble of engines. Other birds simply sing more loudly, and some, such as European robins, sing at night, when the streets are quieter. Because these changes can appear quickly, scientists believe that individual birds learn to modify their songs rather than inheriting new ones.
>
> Such flexibility has limits, however. Singing louder requires more energy, and singing at night can reduce the time available for rest. Moreover, females of some species seem to prefer the lower-pitched songs that city noise drowns out, so males that adapt may be heard but judged less attractive. Species that cannot adjust at all tend to disappear from the noisiest neighborhoods. As a result, urban noise may gradually reduce the variety of birds that live alongside people.

### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 46-50)

> **The Decoy Effect**
>
> When shoppers choose between two products, their decision can be changed by adding a third option that almost no one wants. Marketers call this the decoy effect. The decoy is not really meant to be sold; its purpose is to make one of the other options look better by comparison.
>
> A simple illustration involves drink sizes. Suppose a café sells a small coffee for $3 and a large one for $5. Many customers will choose the small. If the café adds a medium for $4.50, however, the large suddenly looks like a bargain, since it costs only slightly more than the medium. Sales of the large coffee typically rise, even though hardly anyone buys the medium. The customers' needs have not changed, yet their preferences have.
>
> Psychologists explain the effect by noting that people rarely judge value in absolute terms. Because it is hard to know what a cup of coffee is truly worth, buyers look for easy comparisons, and the decoy supplies one. Critics argue that the practice manipulates consumers into spending more than they intended. Others reply that customers remain free to choose and that simply knowing about the technique is usually enough to resist it.
