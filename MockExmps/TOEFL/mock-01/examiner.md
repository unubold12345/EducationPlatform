### Academic talk - Marine biology (Questions 25-28)

[F-US] Today I want to introduce the idea of a keystone species. That's an animal that has a much larger effect on its environment than you'd expect from its numbers. A classic example is the sea otter, which lives along the Pacific coast of North America. Sea otters eat a lot of sea urchins. Now, urchins are small, spiny animals that feed on kelp, a giant seaweed that grows in underwater forests. And these kelp forests are home to hundreds of kinds of fish and other animals. So what happens when the otters disappear? Well, we actually know, because in the eighteen hundreds, hunters killed most of them for their fur. Without otters, the number of urchins exploded. The urchins ate the kelp right down to the sea floor, and they left behind what scientists call urchin barrens, which are basically underwater deserts. The fish that depended on the kelp went away, too. Later, when otters were protected and returned to some areas, the kelp forests grew back, and so did the fish. In places where otters never returned, the barrens are still there. So the otter doesn't just live in the kelp forest. In a sense, it keeps the forest alive. Take it away, and the whole system changes.

### Academic talk - History (Questions 29-32)

[M-AU] Here's a question. When it's noon in one city, what time is it in a city three hundred kilometers to the west? Today, that's easy to answer. But in the early eighteen hundreds, it wasn't. Back then, every town set its clocks by the sun. Noon was simply the moment when the sun was highest in the sky. That meant noon in one town came a few minutes earlier or later than noon in the next town. And for most of history, nobody cared, because nobody could travel fast enough to notice. Then came the railroads. Suddenly, people could pass through several towns in a single hour, and every station kept its own local time. Imagine trying to read a train schedule! In the United States, railroad companies had to deal with dozens of different local times, and mistakes led to missed connections and even accidents. So, in eighteen eighty-three, the railroad companies agreed to divide the country into a small number of time zones. Inside each zone, every clock would show the same time. Some people complained that the railroads were changing the natural time, but the system was so practical that cities quickly adopted it. The following year, an international conference began extending the idea to the rest of the world.

### Academic talk - Psychology (Questions 40-43)

[M-UK] Let's say you have five hours to prepare for an exam. You could study for five hours the night before, or you could study one hour a day for five days. Same amount of time. Which is better? Well, more than a century of research gives a clear answer: spreading it out wins, and it isn't close. Psychologists call this the spacing effect. It was first described in the eighteen eighties by Hermann Ebbinghaus, who tested himself on lists of meaningless syllables, and it's been confirmed in hundreds of studies since. Why does it work? One explanation is that forgetting is actually useful. When you come back to material after a gap, you've forgotten some of it, so your brain has to work to rebuild the memory. That effort is what strengthens it. When you reread your notes five times in one night, by contrast, everything feels familiar, but that feeling is misleading. You're recognizing the material, not recalling it. And here's the irony. When students are asked which method works better, most choose cramming. It feels more productive, because the information is right there, fresh in your mind. Unfortunately, a week later, most of it is gone. So if you remember one thing from today, ideally after a short delay, it's this: study less at a time, but more often.

### Academic talk - Art history (Questions 44-47)

[F-AU] When you picture an ancient Greek statue, you probably imagine smooth white marble. That image is so familiar that, for centuries, people assumed the Greeks wanted their statues white. They didn't. Most ancient statues were originally painted, often in bright blues, reds, and yellows, with detailed patterns on the clothing and even colored eyes and lips. So where did the color go? Well, paint is fragile. After two thousand years of sun, rain, and burial in the ground, most of it simply wore away. And here's an uncomfortable detail. When traces of paint did survive, some collectors in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries actually cleaned them off, because by then pure white marble had come to represent classical beauty. In other words, the ancient look we admire is partly a modern creation. How do we know what the originals looked like? Researchers shine ultraviolet light on the stone, which makes invisible traces of pigment glow. They also analyze tiny particles to identify the exact minerals that were used. From that evidence, they've built full-color reconstructions. Now, many visitors find these reconstructions, frankly, ugly. Too bright, almost like cartoons. But ask yourself whether that reaction tells us about the Greeks' taste or about our own. That's a question I'd like you to think about for next week.
