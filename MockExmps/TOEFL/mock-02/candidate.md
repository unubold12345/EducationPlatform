### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 31-35)

> **Gardens Above the Street**
>
> A green roof is a rooftop that is partly or completely covered with living plants. Beneath the plants lie several layers: soil or a lighter growing material, a drainage layer, and a waterproof sheet that protects the building. Grass-covered roofs are centuries old, but the modern version was developed in Germany in the second half of the twentieth century and has since spread worldwide.
>
> The benefits are considerable. During a storm, the soil and plants hold much of the rain and release it slowly, which reduces the pressure on city drains and lowers the risk of flooded streets. In summer, a planted roof stays far cooler than a dark conventional one, so the rooms below need less air conditioning. Green roofs also offer food and shelter to insects and birds in neighborhoods with little open land.
>
> Nevertheless, green roofs are not suitable everywhere. Wet soil is heavy, and many older buildings would have to be strengthened before they could carry it. A green roof also costs more to install than an ordinary one, and its plants need occasional care. For these reasons, some cities offer tax reductions or direct payments to owners who install them, while others require them on large new buildings. Supporters argue that the long-term savings outweigh the initial expense.

### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 46-50)

Read an academic passage.

> **Did Cooking Make Us Human?**
>
> The human brain is an expensive organ. Although it makes up only about two percent of body weight, it consumes roughly a fifth of the energy the body uses at rest. How our ancestors managed to feed such a demanding organ is a long-standing puzzle. One influential answer, proposed by the primatologist Richard Wrangham, is that they learned to cook.
>
> Heat softens food and begins to break it down before it is eaten. As a result, the body obtains more energy from a cooked meal than from the same meal raw and spends less effort chewing and digesting it. Wild chimpanzees spend several hours a day simply chewing. According to Wrangham, cooking freed early humans from this burden, and the surplus energy allowed the brain to expand. He links the change to Homo erectus, a species that appeared nearly two million years ago with smaller teeth and a larger brain than its predecessors.
>
> Skeptics point to a gap in the record. Clear traces of controlled fire, such as hearths, are much younger than the first Homo erectus fossils. Some researchers therefore credit other changes, including eating more meat or pounding food with stone tools, for the early growth of the brain. The debate remains open, yet few doubt that cooking, whenever it began, reshaped the human body.
