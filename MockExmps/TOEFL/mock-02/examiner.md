### Academic talk - Astronomy (Questions 25-28)

[F-US] Raise your hand if you learned in school that there are nine planets. OK, quite a few of you. So what happened to number nine? Well, Pluto didn't change. Our definition did. Pluto was discovered in nineteen thirty, and for decades it was simply the ninth planet. But it was always a strange one. It's tiny, smaller than our own Moon, and its orbit is tilted compared with the others. Then, in the nineteen nineties, astronomers started finding more and more icy objects out beyond Neptune, in a region called the Kuiper Belt. And in two thousand five, they found one, later named Eris, that's about the same size as Pluto. So here was the problem. If Pluto's a planet, then Eris is too, and maybe dozens of others. Where do you stop? So in two thousand six, the International Astronomical Union voted on an official definition. A planet has to orbit the Sun, it has to be big enough for its gravity to pull it into a round shape, and it has to have cleared other objects out of its path. Pluto passes the first two tests. But it shares its neighborhood with all those icy bodies, so it fails the third. That's why it's now called a dwarf planet. Not everybody was happy about that, by the way, and some astronomers still argue the definition should change.

### Academic talk - Business history (Questions 29-32)

[M-AU] Let's start with a number. Before nineteen thirteen, it took workers at Henry Ford's factory more than twelve hours to assemble the chassis of one Model T, that's the frame with the engine and wheels. About a year later, it took an hour and a half. What changed? Not the car. What changed was how the work moved. Before, the car stayed in one place, and teams of workers walked around it, fetching parts. Ford's engineers turned that around. They put the car on a moving line and had it travel past the workers. Each person stood in one spot and did one small job, over and over, maybe tightening the same two bolts all day. The results were dramatic. Because cars were faster to build, they were cheaper to build, and the price of a Model T fell year after year, until ordinary families could afford one. But there was a cost. The work was so dull and so tiring that workers kept quitting, and Ford had to keep hiring and training new ones. His answer, in nineteen fourteen, was the five-dollar day, roughly double the usual pay. It worked. People stayed. And, as it happened, with wages like that, his own workers could now buy the cars they were building.

### Academic talk - Environmental science (Questions 40-43)

[F-UK] I'd like to clear up a misconception before we go any further. When people see photos of a bleached reef, all that ghostly white, they tend to assume they're looking at dead coral. They're not. Not yet, anyway. Here's what's going on. A coral is an animal, and living inside its tissue are microscopic algae. It's a partnership. The coral provides shelter, and the algae, through photosynthesis, supply most of the coral's food. They're also what gives a reef its color. Now, when the water stays unusually warm for several weeks, even by just a degree or two, that partnership breaks down. The algae start producing substances that are toxic to their host, and the coral responds by, well, evicting them. What's left is transparent tissue over a white skeleton. Hence, bleaching. So a bleached coral is alive, but it's essentially starving. If the water cools in time, the algae come back and the coral recovers. If it doesn't, the coral dies. And this is where I struggle to sound optimistic. A reef can bounce back from one bad summer, but that takes a decade or so. What we're seeing now are heat waves arriving every few years, which simply doesn't leave enough time. So when you read that a reef has recovered, I'd encourage you to ask: recovered for how long?

### Academic talk - Linguistics (Questions 44-47)

[M-AU] Here's something that ought to puzzle us more than it does. Nobody sits a two-year-old down and explains the past tense. And yet, by four or so, virtually every child is using it. So how do they pull that off? The obvious guess is imitation. Children just copy what they hear. But listen to the mistakes they make. A child will say 'I goed to the park' or 'two foots.' Now, no adult says 'goed.' The child can't be copying. What she's done is work out a rule, put a 'd' sound on the end for the past, and apply it a little too enthusiastically. There's a lovely experiment from the nineteen fifties that makes the same point. The researcher, Jean Berko, showed young children a drawing of an imaginary creature and said, 'This is a wug.' Then she showed them two. 'Now there are two...' And the children said 'wugs.' They'd never heard that word in their lives, so they couldn't have memorized its plural. And interestingly, when parents do correct their children, it tends to be about facts, not grammar. Call a dog a cat, and you'll be corrected. Say something true in broken grammar, and you'll get a smile. So children aren't parrots. They're more like little scientists, testing guesses about a system no one has explained to them.
