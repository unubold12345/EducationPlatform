### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 31-35)

Read an academic passage.

> **Why We Put Things Off**
>
> Almost everyone has delayed an important task, such as studying for an exam, even when they knew the delay would cause trouble later. This habit is called procrastination. It is often blamed on laziness or poor time management, but many psychologists now believe that the real cause lies elsewhere.
>
> According to this view, procrastination is mainly a way of managing emotions. A difficult task can produce unpleasant feelings, including boredom, anxiety, and doubt about one's own ability. Turning to something more enjoyable, like watching videos, brings immediate relief. The human mind also tends to value rewards that come now more than rewards that come later, so the comfort of the present moment easily outweighs a distant deadline. The relief is temporary, however, and the unfinished work usually returns with added stress and guilt.
>
> Understanding this cycle suggests practical remedies. Dividing a large project into small steps makes the first step less frightening, and removing distractions, for example by switching off a phone, reduces temptation. Surprisingly, being kind to oneself also seems to help. In one study, students who forgave themselves for delaying their preparation for an exam procrastinated less before the next one. Harsh self-criticism, by contrast, tends to increase the negative feelings that led to the delay in the first place.

### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 46-50)

Read an academic passage.

> **Reading the Rings**
>
> In regions with distinct seasons, a tree adds a new layer of wood beneath its bark every year. Rapid growth in spring produces pale wood, while slower growth late in the summer leaves a thin, dark band. Together, the two form one annual ring, so counting the rings of a trunk reveals the tree's age. The rings record far more than age, however.
>
> Because trees grow more in favorable years, a wide ring generally indicates plentiful rain and a narrow one drought or cold. Trees throughout a region therefore share a similar sequence of wide and narrow rings. By matching the pattern in a living tree with the overlapping pattern in older wood, such as beams from historic buildings, researchers can extend the record back thousands of years. This technique has allowed archaeologists to date ancient wooden structures to the very year their timber was cut, and it has given climate scientists a year-by-year account of past droughts.
>
> The method is not without shortcomings. Many tropical trees, which grow throughout the year, form no clear annual rings. Moreover, a narrow ring is ambiguous: it may reflect a dry year, but an insect attack or the shade of a taller neighbor can stunt growth as well. For this reason, scientists avoid drawing conclusions from a single tree and compare samples from many trees instead.
