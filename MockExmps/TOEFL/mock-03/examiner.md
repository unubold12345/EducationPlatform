### Academic talk - Zoology (Questions 25-28)

[F-UK] OK, let's start with a puzzle. How does an animal catch a tiny flying insect in complete darkness? Bats do it hundreds of times a night. And no, it's not because they have amazing eyes. By the way, bats aren't blind. That's a myth. But in the dark, they rely on something else, called echolocation. Here's how it works. As a bat flies, it sends out very short, very high calls. Most of them are too high for human ears, so to us the night seems quiet. When a call hits an object, say, a moth, an echo bounces back. The bat's brain measures how long that echo took to return. A quick echo means the moth is close. A slower one means it's farther away. And the bat doesn't call at a steady rate. When it's just searching, it might call ten or twenty times a second. But as it closes in on an insect, the calls come faster and faster, up to two hundred a second. Scientists actually call this the feeding buzz. So the bat gets more and more detail exactly when it needs it. Now, the insects aren't helpless. Some moths have ears that can pick up bat calls, and when they hear one, they dive toward the ground. So next time, we'll look at this contest between the hunter and the hunted.

### Academic talk - Economics (Questions 29-32)

[M-UK] Let's begin with a simple question. Why do countries trade at all? Why doesn't each country just make everything it needs? Well, part of the answer is obvious. Bananas grow easily in Ecuador. They don't grow easily in Norway. So Norway buys bananas, and it sells things like fish and oil. But here's the less obvious part. Trade can help both sides even when one country is better at making everything. This idea goes back to David Ricardo, an English economist writing in the early eighteen hundreds. Economists call it comparative advantage. Let me give you an everyday example. Imagine a doctor who also happens to type faster than her assistant. Should she do her own typing? No. Every hour she spends typing is an hour she isn't seeing patients, and that's where she's most valuable. So she hires the assistant, and they're both better off. Countries work the same way. A country gains by concentrating on what it does best, compared with its other options, and trading for the rest. Now, I'm not saying trade is painless. When cheaper imports arrive, some local factories close, and those workers pay a real price. But for the economy as a whole, most economists agree that specializing and trading leaves countries richer than doing everything alone.

### Academic talk - Engineering (Questions 40-43)

[M-US] Here's something that ought to embarrass modern engineers a little. A concrete sea wall built today may start crumbling within fifty years. Yet Roman harbors have sat in salt water for two thousand years, and the Pantheon's concrete dome in Rome is still standing after nearly nineteen centuries. So what did the Romans know that we don't? Well, their recipe was different. Instead of modern cement, they mixed lime with volcanic ash. And in sea water, something remarkable happens. The water seeps in and reacts with the ash, and over time new crystals grow inside the concrete. In other words, the very thing that destroys our concrete made theirs stronger. There's a second clue. Roman concrete is full of tiny white lumps of lime. For years, people wrote these off as sloppy mixing. But recent research suggests they act as a repair kit. When a crack opens and rainwater gets in, it dissolves some of that lime, which then hardens again and seals the crack. Now, before you get carried away, Roman concrete isn't better across the board. It's weaker than ours under heavy loads, and it sets slowly. You couldn't build a skyscraper with it. Still, cement production is a major source of carbon emissions, so a concrete that repairs itself and lasts for centuries is well worth a second look.

### Academic talk - Sociology (Questions 44-47)

[F-AU] Think about the last time you heard about a job opening, or an apartment, or even a good film. Who told you? Your best friend? Possibly. But there's a fair chance it was someone you barely know. Back in nineteen seventy-three, the sociologist Mark Granovetter published a paper with a wonderfully paradoxical title, The Strength of Weak Ties. By strong ties, he meant close friends and family. Weak ties are acquaintances: an old classmate, a neighbor you nod to. Granovetter had asked people near Boston how they'd found their jobs. And of those who'd heard through a personal contact, most said it was someone they saw only occasionally or rarely. Not a close friend. Why would that be? Well, your close friends know one another. They read the same things and hear the same news, so what they tell you is, more often than not, something you already know. An acquaintance, on the other hand, moves in different circles. She's a bridge to information that would never reach you otherwise. Now, I'm not suggesting close friends don't matter. They're the ones who'll help you move house or sit with you when things go wrong. Weak ties won't do that. But when it comes to fresh information, it's the people at the edge of your social world who tend to deliver.
