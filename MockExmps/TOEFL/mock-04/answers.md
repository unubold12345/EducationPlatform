**1-10 (Biology).** The immune system is the body's defense against bacteria, viruses, and other causes of disease. White blood cells travel through the body and search for anything that does not belong. Some of these cells destroy the invader directly, while others produce antibodies that mark it for removal. After an infection, the body keeps special cells that remember the germ, so it can respond faster the next time.

**11-20 (Environmental science).** Renewable energy comes from natural sources that are constantly renewed, such as sunlight, wind, and flowing water. Unlike coal and oil, these sources will not run out in the future. They also release far less carbon dioxide when they are used to make electricity. However, sunshine and wind are not constant, so engineers are developing larger batteries that can store electricity until it is needed.

**36-45 (Economics).** Inflation is a general rise in the prices of goods and services across an economy over time. As prices climb, each unit of currency buys less than it did before. Economists usually measure inflation by tracking the cost of a fixed basket of everyday items. When wages fail to keep pace, households can afford less. Central banks therefore try to keep inflation low and steady, often by raising or lowering interest rates.

## Write for an Academic Discussion - model response (score 5)

> I believe that charging a fee is the better policy, as long as the fee is high enough to matter. Yusuf is right that a very small charge is easy to ignore, but that is an argument for raising the price, not for a ban. Greta mentions that the money can support recycling, and I would add another advantage: a fee is much easier to enforce. When bags are banned completely, some shops keep handing them out secretly, and inspectors must visit every market to stop them. If people are still using too many bags after a year, the government can increase the charge step by step until the habit really changes.

## Take an Interview - model responses (score 5)

**8. Thank you for taking part in this study. Let's start with your own experience. Please tell me about a trip you took that you remember well. Where did you go, and what made it memorable?** (event)

> A trip I remember very well is a visit to my grandparents' village two summers ago. I went with my cousin, and we traveled by train for almost nine hours. The village is in the mountains, so the air was cool and clean, which was a big change from the city. What made the trip memorable was how simple each day was. We helped my grandfather pick apples in the morning, swam in the river in the afternoon, and listened to his stories at night. There was almost no phone signal, so nobody looked at a screen. I came home feeling completely rested.

**9. Some people like to plan every detail of a vacation in advance, while others prefer to decide what to do after they arrive. Which kind of traveler are you, and why?** (life)

> I am definitely a planner, although I leave a little free time. Before a trip, I book my transportation and my accommodation, and I make a short list of places I really want to see. The main reason is money. Tickets and rooms are usually cheaper when you reserve them early, and as a student I have to be careful with my budget. Planning also saves time, because I don't spend half a morning deciding where to go. However, I never fill the whole schedule. I always keep one afternoon open, since the best moments often happen when you simply walk around without a plan.

**10. Now I'd like to ask for your opinion. Some people say that you learn more by traveling to another country than by reading or watching videos about it. Do you agree or disagree? Why?** (opinion)

> I mostly agree. Books and videos can give you facts, but they are chosen and edited by someone else, so you only see what that person wants to show. When you travel, you notice small things that nobody films, such as how people wait in line, how loudly they speak, or what they eat for breakfast. You also have to solve real problems, like buying a ticket in another language, and that teaches you a lot. On the other hand, a short trip can give a false picture, because tourists usually see only the most famous places. So I think travel teaches more, but only if you stay long enough and talk to local people.

**11. Here is my final question. Popular tourist destinations sometimes become so crowded that daily life gets harder for the people who live there. Do you think cities should limit the number of tourists who can visit? Why or why not?** (opinion)

> Yes, I think cities should be able to limit tourist numbers when it is really necessary. In very popular places, residents face higher rents, crowded buses, and noise late at night, and some of them finally move away. If that continues, the city loses the local life that made it attractive in the first place. However, I would not close the city to visitors. A better method is to control the busiest places and times, for example by selling a fixed number of tickets for a famous square or museum each day. Cities can also encourage people to come in the quiet season. In this way, tourism still supports local jobs without making life unpleasant for residents.
