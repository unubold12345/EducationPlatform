### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 31-35)

Read an academic passage.

> **Borrowed Words**
>
> No language is built entirely from its own materials. When speakers of different languages trade, travel, or live side by side, words pass from one language to another. Linguists call these words loanwords, although the term is slightly misleading, since a borrowed word is never given back.
>
> Borrowing usually happens when a language lacks a name for something new. English speakers who first encountered foods such as sushi and yogurt simply adopted the Japanese and Turkish names along with the products. Borrowed words rarely stay unchanged, however. Their pronunciation is adjusted to fit the sound system of the new language, and their form may be shortened. In Japanese, for example, the English word "television" became terebi.
>
> Not all borrowing fills a gap. After the Norman Conquest of 1066, French was the language of England's rulers, and thousands of French words entered English even where native words already existed. This is why the animals in the field are called cow and sheep, both Old English words, while the meat on the table is beef and mutton, both from French. Borrowing, in other words, reflects prestige as well as need, and a language's vocabulary is a record of the peoples its speakers have met.

### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 46-50)

> **Detecting Distant Worlds**
>
> Astronomers long suspected that the Sun was not the only star with planets, but proof was slow to come. A planet shines only by reflected light, and that faint glow is drowned out by the star it circles. Seeing one directly is like trying to spot a firefly beside a lighthouse from many kilometers away. The first planet orbiting a Sun-like star was not confirmed until 1995.
>
> Because direct observation is so difficult, most discoveries have relied on indirect evidence. One approach watches for a wobble: as a planet orbits, its gravity tugs the star slightly back and forth, and this motion subtly shifts the color of the star's light. Another, the transit method, monitors a star's brightness. If a planet happens to pass between the star and Earth, the light dims by a tiny fraction at regular intervals. Space telescopes using this technique have identified thousands of planets.
>
> Neither method is neutral, however. A large planet close to its star produces a stronger wobble and deeper, more frequent dips in brightness than a small, distant one, so giant planets in tight orbits dominated the early lists. Astronomers therefore caution that the catalog reflects the limits of their instruments as much as the true population of planets. Worlds resembling Earth may be common; they are simply harder to detect.
