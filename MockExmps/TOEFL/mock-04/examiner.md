### Academic talk - Psychology (Questions 25-28)

[F-UK] Imagine you have a headache, and I give you a pill. Half an hour later, the pain is gone. Now, what if I told you that pill was made of sugar? Nothing in it at all. Would you believe me? Well, this happens all the time, and psychologists call it the placebo effect. A placebo is a treatment with no active ingredient. What does the work isn't the pill. It's your expectation that you're going to feel better. And here's the part I find fascinating. The details of the treatment matter. In experiments, people report more relief from two sugar pills than from one. A placebo they're told is expensive works better than one they think is cheap. So it's really the meaning we attach to the treatment that counts. Is it all imagination, then? Not exactly. When people expect pain relief, their brains actually release natural painkilling chemicals. So the change is real. But let's be careful here. Placebos mostly affect things we feel, like pain or tiredness. A sugar pill won't mend a broken bone. And this is exactly why researchers testing a new medicine give one group the real drug and another group a placebo. Only if the drug beats the placebo can they say it really works.

### Academic talk - Geography (Questions 29-32)

[M-US] OK, let's start with a little puzzle. In the Sahara, an afternoon can easily reach forty degrees Celsius. But stay there after dark, and the temperature may fall by twenty degrees or more. On a winter night, it can even come close to freezing. So where does all that heat go? Any ideas? Well, the short answer is: straight up. Let me explain. During the day, the ground soaks up energy from the sun. At night, it gives that energy back as heat, which escapes upward. Now, in most places, something keeps a lot of that heat from getting away. Water. Water vapor in the air and clouds overhead absorb the heat and send part of it back down. They work like a blanket. That's why a cloudy night usually feels milder than a clear one. But desert air is extremely dry, and the sky is almost always clear. There's no blanket. So the heat just leaks away into space. And there's a second reason. Sand itself. Sand warms up fast, but only the top layer gets hot, so it doesn't hold much heat, and it loses it quickly once the sun goes down. Compare that with a tropical rainforest. The air there is full of moisture, so the nights are only a little cooler than the days. Same sun, very different nights.

### Academic talk - Economics (Questions 40-43)

[F-AU] Picture a pasture that belongs to no one and is open to every herder in the village. Each herder asks a perfectly reasonable question: should I add one more cow? Well, the profit from that cow is mine alone. The damage it does to the grass, on the other hand, is shared by everybody. So I add the cow. And so does my neighbor, and hers. Before long, the pasture is bare, and everyone is worse off. In nineteen sixty-eight, the ecologist Garrett Hardin called this the tragedy of the commons, and you can see why the label stuck. Swap the pasture for the open ocean, and the cows for fishing boats, and you have a fair description of overfishing. Notice that nobody in the story is a villain. Each person acts sensibly, and together they wreck the thing they depend on. Hardin saw essentially two ways out: divide the resource into private property, or have the government regulate it. Now, that's where I think he was too gloomy. The political scientist Elinor Ostrom spent decades studying real communities, Swiss mountain villages sharing pastures, farmers sharing irrigation water, and she found that many had managed common resources for centuries. They made their own rules, kept an eye on one another, and punished cheats. So the tragedy is a real risk. It just isn't destiny.

### Academic talk - Marine biology (Questions 44-47)

[M-US] Go down a thousand meters into the ocean, and the last trace of sunlight is gone. You'd expect total darkness. And yet, if you switch off your submarine's lights, the water around you flickers and sparkles. That's bioluminescence, light made by living things, and down there it's not the exception. It's the rule. The chemistry is fairly simple. A molecule called luciferin reacts with oxygen, with help from an enzyme, and the energy comes out as light instead of heat. What's more interesting is what the light is for. The anglerfish dangles a glowing lure in front of its mouth, and you can guess how that ends for a curious little fish. Some shrimp do the opposite. When attacked, they spit out a glowing cloud and slip away while the predator is dazzled. Then there's my favorite trick, and for this one we go a little higher, where a faint glow still filters down from the surface. The hatchetfish has lights along its belly. Odd place for them, isn't it? But seen from below, a fish is a dark shape against that glow. By lighting its belly to match, the hatchetfish erases its own shadow. It hides by shining. Most of this light is blue, because blue travels farthest through seawater. A few dragonfish, though, make red light, which most of their neighbors can't see at all. In effect, they hunt with a private flashlight.
