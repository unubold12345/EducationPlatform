### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 31-35)

Read an academic passage.

> **Umami: The Fifth Taste**
>
> For most of the twentieth century, textbooks listed four basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, and bitter. Yet as early as 1908, a Japanese chemist named Kikunae Ikeda had argued for a fifth. He noticed that a traditional broth made from kelp had a rich, savory quality that none of the four could explain. He went on to isolate the substance responsible, an amino acid called glutamate, and named the taste umami.
>
> Many scientists outside Japan doubted that umami was truly distinct. They assumed it was merely a blend of the other tastes. The debate was settled only around 2000, when researchers identified receptors on the tongue that respond specifically to glutamate. Like sweetness, which signals energy, umami appears to have a purpose: it tells the body that a food contains protein.
>
> Cooks made use of umami long before it had a name. Glutamate is plentiful in ripe tomatoes, mushrooms, and aged cheese, and methods such as drying and fermenting release even more of it. That is why a little soy sauce or grated Parmesan can make a simple dish taste fuller. Some food scientists also believe that savory ingredients can keep meals satisfying when less salt is used.

### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 46-50)

> **The First Paper Money**
>
> Paper money was born from an inconvenience. In the Chinese region of Sichuan about a thousand years ago, everyday coins were made of iron, and a large purchase could require a load of coins too heavy to carry. So merchants left their coins with trusted shops and received paper receipts in return. Because a receipt could be exchanged for coins at any time, people soon began to trade the receipts themselves.
>
> In the 1020s the government of the Song dynasty took over the system and began issuing official notes, generally regarded as the first paper money issued by a state. The idea astonished later visitors. When the Venetian traveler Marco Polo described China in the thirteenth century, he wrote with amazement about sheets of paper that people accepted as readily as silver or gold.
>
> Yet the invention carried a danger. Coins are limited by the supply of metal, but paper can be printed at almost no cost. When rulers needed funds, they issued more and more notes, and each note bought less than before. Repeated episodes of this kind eventually led China to abandon paper notes and rely on silver for several centuries. Paper money, it turned out, depends less on paper than on trust in whoever prints it.
