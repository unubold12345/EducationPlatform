### Academic talk - Meteorology (Questions 25-28)

[M-US] OK, so last time we talked about ordinary thunderstorms. Today, something much bigger: hurricanes. And I want you to think of a hurricane as an engine. Like any engine, it needs fuel. So what's the fuel? It's warm ocean water. The sea surface has to be at least about twenty-seven degrees Celsius. That's why these storms are born in the tropics, in late summer and early fall, when the ocean is at its warmest. Here's what happens. Warm, wet air rises from the sea surface. As it rises, it cools, and the water vapor in it turns into cloud droplets. Now, this is the key step. When vapor turns into liquid, it gives off heat. That heat warms the air around it, so the air rises even faster, and more wet air gets pulled in below to replace it. You see? The storm feeds itself. Now, why does it spin? That comes from the rotation of the Earth, which bends the path of the moving air. Interestingly, that effect is too weak right at the equator, so hurricanes don't form there. And what stops the engine? Well, take away the fuel. Once a hurricane moves over land or over cooler water, its supply of warm, wet air is cut off, and it gradually weakens.

### Academic talk - Art history (Questions 29-32)

[F-UK] Take a look at these two paintings. The one on the left is from the thirteen hundreds. Notice anything odd? The most important figure is huge, even though he's standing at the back, and the buildings look as if they might slide off the wall. Now the one on the right, painted about a century later. Suddenly you feel you could walk into the room. So what changed? Painters didn't simply get better at drawing. They learned a system, and we call it linear perspective. The idea is simple. Lines that are parallel in real life, like the edges of a road, seem to come together as they move away from you. In a painting, they meet at a single spot called the vanishing point. And the farther away an object is, the smaller you paint it. We usually give the credit to an architect, Filippo Brunelleschi, working in Florence in the early fourteen hundreds. He painted a building in his city so accurately that people standing in front of it could compare the two, and they matched. Some years later, another architect, Leon Battista Alberti, wrote the rules down, so any painter could learn them. Alberti said a painting should be like an open window. And for the next four hundred years or so, that's pretty much what European painting tried to be.

### Academic talk - Biology (Questions 40-43)

[F-US] If you've ever watched an octopus on a reef, you'll know it can vanish in front of your eyes. One moment it's brown, the next it's the exact pattern of the rock behind it. And we're talking well under a second. So how on earth does it manage that? The secret is in the skin, which is packed with thousands of tiny organs called chromatophores. Picture each one as a little elastic bag of pigment, with a ring of muscles around it. When those muscles tighten, they stretch the bag out flat, and you see a spot of color. When they relax, the bag shrinks to a dot you can barely see. Now, here's the crucial part. Each of those muscles is wired straight to the brain. In many other animals that change color, the signal is chemical. It travels in the blood, so it's slow. The octopus uses nerves, and that's why the change is practically instant. But there's a twist, and frankly, it still bothers researchers. As far as we can tell, octopuses are color-blind. Their eyes have just one kind of light-detecting cell. So how do they match colors they can't see? One suggestion is that the skin itself detects light. It's an attractive idea, but I'd call it unproven for now.

### Academic talk - Anthropology (Questions 44-47)

[M-UK] Let me start with a confession. Last week a neighbor brought me a jar of homemade jam, and my first thought wasn't 'how nice.' It was, 'Now I owe her something.' Well, according to the French scholar Marcel Mauss, that reaction is the whole point. In a famous essay from the nineteen twenties, Mauss argued that there's no such thing as a free gift. A gift carries three duties: to give, to accept, and, sooner or later, to give something back. Some of his best-known evidence came from the Trobriand Islands, near New Guinea. There, men sailed long distances to exchange shell necklaces and armbands with partners on other islands. Now, these objects were of no practical use. Nobody kept one for long, either. You held it for a while, then passed it on. So what was the point? The point was the partnership itself. Each exchange renewed a bond between two people, sometimes for life, and ordinary trade in food and tools followed along behind it. Compare that with buying something in a shop. You pay, you leave, and you and the cashier are even. A gift does the opposite. It leaves a debt hanging, and that debt is precisely what holds people together. So the next time you get a present you didn't ask for, well, you'll know what you've signed up for.
