### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 31-35)

> **Two Languages in One Mind**
>
> For a long time, many people assumed that bilingual speakers kept their two languages in separate mental boxes and simply opened one at a time. Recent research has shown that this picture is wrong. When bilinguals hear or read a word in one language, related words in their other language become active as well, even if that language is not being used.
>
> This means that a bilingual brain is constantly managing competition. To speak in one language, it must select the right words and hold back those from the other. Some researchers believe that this daily practice strengthens general mental skills, such as focusing attention and switching between tasks. In several experiments, bilingual children and adults performed slightly better than monolinguals on tasks that required ignoring distracting information.
>
> The evidence is not conclusive, however. Other studies, including some with very large groups of participants, have found no such advantage, and the debate continues. Managing two languages also has small costs. Bilinguals usually know fewer words in each language than people who speak only that language, and they more often feel that a word is "on the tip of the tongue." Most researchers agree that these costs are minor compared with the benefit of being able to communicate in two languages.

### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 46-50)

> **Learning from Nature's Designs**
>
> Engineers facing a difficult problem sometimes look for an answer in an unexpected place: the natural world. This approach is known as biomimicry. Over millions of years, evolution has tested countless designs, and those that survive tend to be efficient. Rather than starting from nothing, a designer can study how a plant or animal has already solved a similar problem.
>
> The best-known example is Velcro. In the 1940s, the Swiss engineer George de Mestral noticed that seeds stuck to his dog's fur after a walk. Under a microscope, he saw that each seed was covered with tiny hooks, and he copied the idea to create a new fastener. Decades later, engineers in Japan reshaped the nose of a high-speed train to resemble the beak of a kingfisher, a bird that dives into water with barely a splash. The redesigned train was quieter when it left tunnels and consumed less electricity.
>
> Biomimicry is not a shortcut, however. Nature builds with materials and methods that factories cannot easily reproduce; spider silk, for instance, is remarkably strong for its weight, yet producing it in large quantities has proved difficult. Moreover, living things are shaped by the need to survive and reproduce, not to serve human purposes, so a natural design usually has to be adapted rather than simply copied.
