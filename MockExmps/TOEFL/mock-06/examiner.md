### Academic talk - History (Questions 25-28)

[F-UK] Today's story starts with a vegetable. I want to argue that the potato, yes, the ordinary potato, changed the history of Europe. Potatoes come from the Andes mountains of South America, where people had grown them for thousands of years. Spanish ships carried them to Europe in the fifteen hundreds. And at first? Almost nobody wanted them. They looked strange, they grew underground, and they belong to the same plant family as some poisonous species, so people didn't trust them. For a long time, in many places, potatoes were mostly fed to animals. So what changed people's minds? Hunger, mainly. A field of potatoes can feed far more people than the same field planted with wheat. Potatoes also grow in poor soil and in cool, wet weather, which describes a lot of northern Europe. And here's a detail I like. In wartime, soldiers could easily carry off the grain stored in a farmer's barn, but potatoes left in the ground were much harder to take. By the seventeen hundreds, rulers such as Frederick the Great of Prussia were actually ordering farmers to plant them. The result was a better-fed population that grew quickly, and many historians think that this growth supplied the workers for Europe's new factories and cities. Not bad for a vegetable nobody wanted.

### Academic talk - Chemistry (Questions 29-32)

[M-AU] Let's start with something you've all seen: ice cubes floating in a glass of water. It looks completely ordinary, right? But it's actually strange. With almost every other substance, the solid form is denser than the liquid, so the solid sinks. Drop a piece of solid wax into melted wax, and down it goes. So why is water different? It comes down to the shape of the water molecule and the way these molecules attract one another. In liquid water, the molecules are constantly moving, sliding past each other, and they can crowd fairly close together. But when water freezes, each molecule locks onto its neighbors in a fixed, open pattern, a bit like people holding hands at arm's length. That pattern has a lot of empty space in it. So the same amount of water takes up about nine percent more room as ice, which makes ice less dense than the water around it. Now, why should you care? Well, think about a lake in winter. Because ice floats, the lake freezes from the top down, and that layer of ice works like a blanket. The water underneath stays liquid, and fish survive until spring. If ice sank, lakes could freeze solid from the bottom up. Life in cold regions would look very different.

### Academic talk - Economics (Questions 40-43)

[M-US] Suppose I gave you the only telephone in the world. What would it be worth to you? Nothing, obviously. There'd be nobody to call. Give a second person a phone, and yours suddenly has some value. Add a million people, and it becomes something you can hardly live without. Economists call this a network effect: a product becomes more valuable to each user as the number of users grows. Notice how odd that is. With most goods, it makes no difference to me how many other people own one. My umbrella keeps me just as dry whether or not you have an umbrella too. Now, network effects have a couple of consequences worth remembering. First, getting started is brutally hard. Nobody wants to join an empty network, which is why new platforms so often give their service away for free at the beginning. They're buying a crowd. Second, once a network gets big enough, it tends to pull ahead and stay ahead. A competitor might offer something technically superior and still fail, because what people are really paying for isn't the technology. It's access to everyone else. So when you hear that a market has ended up with one giant firm, don't assume the best product won. Sometimes it's simply the one that got big first.

### Academic talk - Literature (Questions 44-47)

[F-US] The Iliad runs to more than fifteen thousand lines, and it took shape before the Greeks were widely using writing. So how on earth did anyone remember it? For a long time, the assumption was that poets simply had extraordinary memories and recited the poem word for word. Then, in the nineteen thirties, a young American scholar named Milman Parry tried something different. Instead of only studying old texts, he traveled to the Balkans, where singers still performed long heroic poems without being able to read, and he recorded them. What he found was that the singers weren't reciting from memory at all. They were composing as they sang. How? They'd absorbed a huge stock of ready-made phrases, each one shaped to fit the rhythm of a line. Think of Homer's 'swift-footed Achilles' or 'rosy-fingered dawn.' Readers today may find those repetitions tedious, but for the poet, they were building blocks. Whole scenes, like a feast, followed familiar patterns too. And here's the part I find most striking. The singers insisted that they sang a story exactly the same way every time. The recordings showed otherwise: no two performances matched. For them, 'the same' meant the same story, not the same words. So perhaps we should think of an epic less as a fixed text and more as a tradition of performances.
