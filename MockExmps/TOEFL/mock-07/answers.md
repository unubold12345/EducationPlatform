**1-10 (Geology).** An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground that starts at a fault, a deep crack in the Earth's crust. The rocks on each side of a fault are pushed in different directions, but friction holds them in place. Stress builds for many years until the rocks suddenly slip, and the stored energy travels outward as waves. Scientists record these waves with seismographs, but nobody can yet predict exactly when an earthquake will strike.

**11-20 (Health science).** Vitamins are substances that the human body needs in very small amounts in order to stay healthy. Because the body cannot make most of them, people must get them from food. Vitamin C, found in fresh fruit, helps wounds heal, while vitamin D keeps bones strong. Long before vitamins were discovered, sailors who spent months at sea without fruit or vegetables often developed a disease called scurvy.

**36-45 (History).** Hieroglyphs were the picture signs that people in ancient Egypt used for writing. Each sign was a small picture, and it could stand for a whole word or just for a sound. The Egyptians carved these signs into stone walls and wrote them on paper made from a river plant. The skill of reading them was later lost, until a French scholar worked out their meaning in 1822 with the help of the Rosetta Stone.

**36-45 (Economics).** Globalization is the process by which the economies of different countries become increasingly connected through trade, investment, and technology. Falling transport costs and faster communication have allowed companies to manufacture goods wherever production is cheapest. As a result, consumers often enjoy lower prices and greater variety, although workers in some industries have lost their jobs to foreign competition. Economists therefore continue to debate how the gains from open markets should be shared.

## Build a Sentence

1. **No, she does not like loud music.** (negation)
2. **Is your new apartment far from campus?** (yes no question)
3. **Which topic did you choose for your talk?** (wh question)
4. **The evening class is less crowded than the morning one.** (comparison)

## Take an Interview - model responses (score 5)

**8. Thank you for joining me today. I'd like to start with your own experience. Think of a friend you don't see very often. How did the two of you meet, and how do you stay in contact now?** (event)

> One friend I don't see very often is Mateo. We met on the first day of high school, because the teacher gave us seats next to each other. We soon discovered that we supported the same football team, and after that we spent almost every weekend together. Two years ago, he moved to another city to study engineering, so now we mostly keep in touch online. We send each other short voice messages a few times a week, usually about football or something funny that happened. About once a month we have a long video call, and during the holidays we always try to meet in person.

**9. Some people like to have a large circle of friends, while others prefer just a few close ones. Which is closer to your own preference, and why?** (life)

> I definitely prefer having a few close friends. For me, friendship takes time, and I only have a limited amount of it because of my studies and my part-time job. If I tried to stay close to twenty people, I would not be able to give any of them real attention. With my three best friends, I can talk about serious things, such as family problems or worries about the future, and I know they will listen. Of course, I enjoy meeting new people in class, and I am friendly with many of them. But I would call them acquaintances rather than true friends.

**10. Now I'd like to hear your opinion. Some people believe that a friendship cannot really stay strong when two friends live far away from each other. Do you agree or disagree? Why?** (opinion)

> I disagree with that view. Distance certainly makes a friendship harder, because you cannot share everyday moments, like eating lunch together. However, I think the strength of a friendship depends on effort, not on location. My cousin and her best friend have lived in different countries for ten years, and they are still very close because they call each other every Sunday. In some ways, distance can even improve a friendship, since people stop taking each other for granted. On the other hand, if neither person makes time for the other, the friendship will fade, even if they live on the same street.

**11. Here is my last question. Many students move to a new city for university and find it hard to make friends there. Do you think universities should do more to help new students build friendships, or is that the students' own responsibility? Please explain.** (opinion)

> I think universities should do more, although students also have to make an effort. Starting university can be very lonely, especially for students who come from another city or country and know nobody. Loneliness affects their health and their grades, so it is in the university's interest to help. For example, universities could organize small group activities in the first weeks, such as hiking trips or cooking evenings, instead of only big welcome parties where shy people feel lost. They could also match each new student with an older student from the same department. After that first step, it is up to the students to keep the friendships going.
