### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 31-35)

Read an academic passage.

> **How Habits Take Hold**
>
> A habit is a behavior that a person performs almost automatically, with little conscious thought. Brushing one's teeth, locking the door, and checking a phone on waking are familiar examples. Psychologists describe a habit as a loop with three parts: a cue that triggers the behavior, the routine itself, and a reward that makes the brain want to repeat it.
>
> Habits form through repetition in a stable setting. Each time a routine follows the same cue and brings a reward, the link between them grows stronger, until the cue alone is enough to start the action. This takes longer than many people assume. In one well-known study at a London university, volunteers needed about two months, on average, before a new daily behavior felt automatic, and some needed far longer. Missing a single day, however, did not noticeably slow their progress.
>
> Because habits are tied to cues, they are hard to break by willpower alone. An old routine tends to return whenever the familiar cue appears, especially when a person is tired or under stress. For this reason, psychologists often advise changing the surroundings instead: keeping snacks out of sight, for instance, or taking a different route home. Research on people who have just moved suggests the same thing. In a new place, the old cues are missing, so old routines are easier to replace.

### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 46-50)

> **Penicillin and the Rise of Resistance**
>
> In 1928, the Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming returned from a holiday to find that mold had grown on one of the dishes in which he was raising bacteria. Around the mold, the bacteria had died. Fleming named the substance responsible penicillin, but he could not produce enough of it to treat patients. That step was taken more than a decade later by a team at Oxford that included Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, and by the mid-1940s factories were turning out the drug in enormous quantities.
>
> Penicillin transformed medicine. Infections that had often been fatal could now be cured within days. Yet Fleming himself sounded a warning. Accepting the Nobel Prize in 1945, he pointed out that careless use of the drug might allow bacteria to become resistant to it.
>
> His fear proved justified. In any large population of bacteria, a few may happen to carry a trait that lets them survive a drug. When an antibiotic kills their competitors, these survivors multiply and pass the trait on. The more widely antibiotics are used, therefore, the more chances resistant strains have to spread. Today some infections no longer respond to drugs that once cured them easily, and few new antibiotics have reached the market in recent decades. The discovery that seemed to defeat bacterial disease turned out to be only the opening move in a much longer contest.
