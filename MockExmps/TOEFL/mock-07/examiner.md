### Academic talk - Architecture (Questions 25-28)

[F-AU] Here's something that might surprise you. If you stand at the top of a very tall building on a windy day, the floor under your feet is moving. Skyscrapers sway. The very tallest can move a meter or more in a storm, and they're designed to. A tower that can bend a little is actually safer than one that can't. So the real problem for engineers isn't keeping the building up. It's keeping the people inside comfortable, because nobody wants to feel seasick at their desk. So, how do they do it? One answer is shape. When wind flows around a square tower, it breaks into swirls of air that push the building from side to side. Rounded corners, or an outline that changes as the tower rises, break up those swirls before they get strong. The Burj Khalifa in Dubai does exactly that. Its outline keeps changing on the way up, so the wind never gets organized. A second answer is weight. Near the top of Taipei 101, in Taiwan, there's an enormous steel ball, more than six hundred tons of it, hanging from cables. When the wind pushes the tower one way, the ball swings the other way and absorbs much of the movement. And it isn't hidden. Visitors can go and look at it. For next time, I'd like each of you to find one more building that uses a trick like this.

### Academic talk - Agriculture (Questions 29-32)

[M-UK] Today's topic is a farming idea that's thousands of years old and still in use: crop rotation. The idea is simple. You don't grow the same crop in the same field year after year. Why not? Well, think about what happens if you plant wheat again and again. Wheat takes a lot of nitrogen out of the soil, so each harvest gets a little smaller. And the insects and diseases that like wheat stay right there in the field, waiting for next year's meal. Now, farmers long ago knew nothing about nitrogen, but they could see the results. In medieval Europe, many villages divided their land into three big fields, and each year one of the three was left empty to rest. It worked. But notice the cost. A third of the land grew nothing. The real breakthrough, which spread across England in the seventeen hundreds, was a four-year rotation. Two of the years were for grain. In the other two, farmers grew turnips and clover. No resting year at all. How was that possible? Clover belongs to the same family as peas and beans, and bacteria living on the roots of these plants take nitrogen from the air and put it back into the soil. So the clover was feeding the field. And the turnips and clover also fed the farm animals through the winter, which meant more animals and more manure for the soil. More food from the same land. Not bad for a simple change of plan.

### Academic talk - Physics (Questions 40-43)

[F-UK] Why is the sky blue? It sounds like a child's question, and that's exactly why I like it, because most adults can't answer it. A surprising number will tell you, quite confidently, that the sky reflects the ocean. It doesn't. So let's do this properly. Sunlight looks white, but it's really a mixture of every color, and each color travels as a wave of a different length. Red waves are long. Blue and violet waves are short. Now, the air is full of gas molecules, far smaller than any of those waves, and when sunlight hits them, it gets scattered, sent off in all directions. But not equally. Short waves are scattered far more strongly than long ones. So blue light ends up bouncing around the whole sky and reaching your eyes from every direction, while most of the red passes straight through. We call this Rayleigh scattering, after the British physicist who worked out the mathematics in the nineteenth century. Now, some of you are already thinking, hang on, violet is even shorter than blue, so why isn't the sky violet? Good. Partly, the sun gives off less violet to begin with, and partly, our eyes just aren't very sensitive to it. And sunsets? At the end of the day, sunlight has to cross far more air to reach you. By then, most of the blue has been scattered out of the beam, and what's left is orange and red.

### Academic talk - Linguistics (Questions 44-47)

[M-US] There are roughly seven thousand languages spoken in the world today. By the end of this century, by many estimates, around half of them will have fallen silent. Now, I want to be careful here, because people tend to picture this the wrong way. Languages rarely die because their speakers die. They die because parents, usually for perfectly sensible reasons, stop passing them on. If the language of school, of work, of the city is something else, then speaking that other language to your children looks like giving them a head start. Nobody decides to kill a language. It just isn't handed down. And one generation is all it takes. So what, you might say. Wouldn't life be simpler with fewer languages? Well, consider what goes with them. A community that has lived in one place for centuries has names for plants, for animals, for kinds of weather, that often exist nowhere else and were never written down. And for us linguists, every language is a piece of evidence about what the human mind can do. Lose it, and that evidence is gone for good. It isn't hopeless, though. In New Zealand in the nineteen eighties, Māori communities began running preschools, called language nests, where small children heard nothing but Māori all day. The idea has since been copied elsewhere, including Hawaii. It doesn't guarantee survival. But it does show that the decline is not a law of nature.
