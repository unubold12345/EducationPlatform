### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 31-35)

> **Third Places**
>
> Most people divide their days between two settings: home, sometimes called the first place, and work or school, the second place. In the late 1980s, the American sociologist Ray Oldenburg argued that healthy communities also need third places: cafés, barbershops, public libraries, parks, and similar spots where people gather informally, simply because they enjoy the company.
>
> Such places share several features. Nobody is obliged to be there, and nobody has to play host. Conversation is the main activity, and a visitor's job title or income counts for little; a student and a company director may share the same table. Prices are low, the doors stay open for long hours, and a group of regulars gives each place its character. Because visitors keep meeting people outside their own family and profession, third places spread news, build trust among neighbors, and help newcomers feel that they belong.
>
> Many observers worry that these settings are disappearing. In suburbs built around the car, there is often nowhere to walk to, and chain stores are usually designed to move customers through quickly rather than to let them linger. Online forums offer a partial substitute, since they allow strangers to talk freely. Critics point out, however, that a screen cannot fully replace the chance meetings that happen when people share a physical space.

### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 46-50)

> **The Microbes Within**
>
> The human body is never truly alone. Trillions of bacteria inhabit the skin, the mouth, and above all the large intestine, forming a community that scientists call the microbiome. For much of the twentieth century, bacteria were regarded chiefly as agents of disease, to be eliminated wherever possible. That view has changed dramatically: most of the microbes we carry are harmless, and many are indispensable.
>
> Gut bacteria perform tasks that human cells cannot. They break down plant fibers that our own enzymes cannot digest, releasing compounds that nourish the cells lining the intestine. They manufacture certain vitamins, including vitamin K. Simply by occupying space and consuming nutrients, they also leave little room for harmful species to gain a foothold. In early childhood, moreover, contact with these residents appears to teach the immune system to distinguish dangerous invaders from harmless ones.
>
> This partnership can be disturbed. Antibiotics, although they save lives, do not discriminate between harmful bacteria and beneficial ones, and a course of treatment may reduce the diversity of the gut community for months. Researchers have linked low diversity to a range of conditions, yet they caution that such links do not prove that one causes the other. Whether an altered microbiome produces an illness or merely accompanies it remains, in many cases, an open question.
