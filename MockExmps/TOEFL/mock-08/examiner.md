### Academic talk - History of technology (Questions 25-28)

[F-AU] Imagine you're the captain of a ship in the Mediterranean about a thousand years ago. How do you know which way you're heading? Well, by day you watch the coast and the sun, and at night you use the stars. That works fine until the sky clouds over. So what did captains do in winter, when it could be cloudy for weeks? Mostly, they stayed home. Ships sat in the harbor for months. Now, the magnetic compass changed that. It came from China, where people had known for centuries that a magnetic stone, left free to turn, always settles in the same direction. At first it was used on land, for things like laying out buildings, not for travel. But by the early eleven hundreds, Chinese sailors were using it at sea, and by the end of that century it shows up in European writings too. And here's what I want you to notice. The compass didn't make ships any faster or any bigger. What it gave sailors was confidence. They could hold a course under a gray sky, out of sight of land. So the sailing season grew longer, merchants could fit more voyages into a year, and trade expanded. Later, of course, the same little needle helped sailors cross whole oceans. But that's next week's story.

### Academic talk - Psychology (Questions 29-32)

[M-UK] OK, quick question. Would you rather pick a dessert from a menu of five or a menu of fifty? Most of us would say fifty. More choice is better, right? Well, let me tell you about a famous study published in the year two thousand. Two psychologists, Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper, set up a tasting table in a grocery store. Sometimes the table had twenty-four kinds of jam. Sometimes it had only six. Now, the big display attracted more shoppers. People love to look. But when it came to actually buying, about thirty percent of the people who saw six jams bought a jar. With twenty-four jams? About three percent. So what's going on? One idea is that comparing takes effort, and when there's too much to compare, we give up and walk away. Another is regret. With twenty-three jams you didn't pick, it's easy to imagine that one of them was better. Now, I should be honest with you. Later studies haven't always found this effect. It seems to appear mainly when the options are hard to tell apart and you don't already know what you like. So I wouldn't say choice is bad. I'd say that past a certain point, adding options stops helping, and sometimes it gets in the way.

### Academic talk - Environmental science (Questions 40-43)

[M-US] If you've ever walked out of a city park on a summer evening and felt the air get warmer within a block or two, you've met today's topic in person. It's called the urban heat island effect: cities tend to be warmer than the countryside around them. It's hardly a new discovery, by the way. An amateur weather-watcher named Luke Howard measured it in London some two hundred years ago. So why does it happen? Partly it's the materials. Asphalt and dark roofs soak up sunlight all day, and then, crucially, they give that heat back after sunset. That's why the gap between city and countryside is usually widest at night, not at noon, which surprises people. Then there's what's missing. Plants cool the air as water evaporates from their leaves, so when you swap fields for pavement, you lose that free air conditioning. And tall buildings on narrow streets trap warmth that would otherwise escape to the sky. Finally, there's the heat we produce ourselves, from engines and, ironically, from air conditioners, which cool the inside of a building by dumping heat outside. So the hotter a city gets, the more it heats itself. What can planners do? Planting trees and using paler, more reflective surfaces both help. Neither is a miracle cure, but in a heat wave, even a degree or two matters.

### Academic talk - Paleontology (Questions 44-47)

[F-UK] Let me start with a slightly depressing fact. Almost nothing that has ever lived became a fossil. When an animal dies, it's normally eaten, or it rots, or the weather breaks down its bones. For a fossil to form, a whole chain of unlikely things has to go right. First, the body needs to be buried quickly, say, by mud at the bottom of a lake or by sand in a riverbed. That keeps scavengers away and cuts off oxygen, which slows decay. Even then, the soft parts usually vanish. It's the hard parts, bones, teeth, and shells, that last. Next, over a very long time, water moving through the sediment carries dissolved minerals into the tiny spaces inside a bone. Bit by bit, the minerals fill those spaces and may replace the original material, until what's left is, in effect, a copy in stone. And we're not done. That stone has to survive millions of years underground, and then erosion has to bring it back to the surface just when somebody happens to walk by. Now, think about what that means for the evidence. Which creatures are we most likely to find? Those with hard parts that lived in or near water. A jellyfish, or a bird in a mountain forest, had far worse odds. So the fossil record isn't a photograph of past life. It's more like a guest list with most of the names missing.
