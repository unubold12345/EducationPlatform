### Read an Academic Passage (Questions 31-35)

Read an academic passage.

> **How the Weekend Was Invented**
>
> A two-day break at the end of every week feels natural today, yet it is a fairly recent invention. In early industrial Britain, factories ran six days a week, and Sunday was the only day off. Many workers, especially skilled craftsmen who were paid for each item they made, simply stayed away on Monday as well. This unofficial holiday became so common that it earned a nickname: "Saint Monday."
>
> Factory owners disliked the custom because they could never predict how many workers would appear. From the middle of the nineteenth century, some of them proposed a trade: if employees came reliably on Monday, they could leave early on Saturday afternoon. The half-day proved popular, and other businesses soon profited from it. Railway companies sold cheap tickets for Saturday trips to the seaside, and football matches were scheduled for Saturday afternoons.
>
> The full two-day weekend arrived later. In 1926, the carmaker Henry Ford gave his factory workers both Saturday and Sunday off without cutting their pay. His motive was partly commercial: people with free time, he reasoned, would spend more money, including on cars. During the economic crisis of the 1930s, shorter weeks were also seen as a way to share scarce work among more people, and by 1940 the forty-hour week had become the legal standard in the United States.

> **Rewiring the Brain**
>
> For most of the twentieth century, scientists assumed that the adult brain was essentially fixed. Children's brains developed rapidly, the thinking went, but once a person reached adulthood, the wiring was complete and could only decline. Research over the past few decades has overturned this view. The brain, it turns out, keeps reorganizing itself throughout life, a capacity known as neuroplasticity.
>
> A well-known study of London taxi drivers illustrates the idea. To earn a license, drivers there must memorize thousands of streets, a task that takes years. Brain scans showed that part of the hippocampus, a region involved in spatial memory, was larger in these drivers than in other adults, and the longer they had been driving, the greater the difference. Plasticity also explains why some patients recover abilities after a stroke: with intensive practice, healthy areas can take over tasks once handled by damaged ones.
>
> Yet rewiring is neither effortless nor always beneficial. Connections that are not used grow weaker, so skills fade without practice. The same mechanism can also strengthen unwanted patterns, as when the nervous system "learns" to keep signaling pain after an injury has healed. Nor does every claim made in the name of plasticity deserve trust: commercial "brain-training" games improve performance on the games themselves, but evidence that they sharpen thinking in general remains weak.
