### Academic talk - Botany (Questions 25-28)

[F-AU] Plants have a problem that animals don't. When something comes along to eat them, they can't run away. So, how does a plant protect itself? Well, the first line of defense is physical. Think of the thorns on a rose, or the tough, waxy surface of a holly leaf. Some leaves are even covered in tiny hairs that make it hard for small insects to walk on them. The second line is chemical. Many plants fill their leaves with substances that taste bitter or are actually poisonous. Caffeine is a good example. We enjoy it in coffee and tea, but the plant makes it to poison insects that chew on its leaves. Now, here's the part I find most impressive. Some plants call for help. When caterpillars start chewing on a corn plant, the damaged leaves release a scent into the air. That scent attracts a certain kind of wasp, and the wasps attack the caterpillars. In other words, the plant is sending out a signal that says, 'Your lunch is over here.' And all of this costs the plant energy, which is why many plants don't produce these chemicals all the time. They wait until they're actually attacked. So next time you walk past a quiet green field, remember, it's not as peaceful as it looks.

### Academic talk - Marketing (Questions 29-32)

[M-US] Here's a price tag that every one of you has seen a thousand times. A shirt costs nineteen ninety-nine. Not twenty dollars. Why? It's only one cent. Well, marketers have a name for this: charm pricing. And there are two main reasons it works. The first is the way we read numbers. We read from left to right, and our brain starts judging the price as soon as it sees the first digit. So nineteen ninety-nine gets filed under 'nineteen-something,' not 'twenty.' Researchers call this the left-digit effect. And notice, it only works when the first digit changes. Dropping a price from three sixty to three fifty-nine does almost nothing. The second reason is what a nine signals. Over the years, shoppers have learned to connect prices ending in nine with sales and discounts. In one well-known experiment, a clothing catalog offered the same dress at thirty-four, thirty-nine, and forty-four dollars. Which sold best? The thirty-nine-dollar one. It even beat the cheaper price. Now, does that mean every store should use nines? Not quite. Have a look at the menu in an expensive restaurant, or the price tag on a luxury watch. You'll usually see round numbers there. A round price says quality, and a nine says discount. So the right ending depends on what you want your customers to think.

### Academic talk - Astronomy (Questions 40-43)

[M-UK] So here's the obvious problem. A black hole, by definition, gives off no light. Its gravity is so strong that nothing that falls in can get back out, not even light itself. So how on earth do you find something you can't see? The short answer is, you watch what it does to its surroundings. One approach is to track stars. At the center of our galaxy, astronomers spent years following a handful of stars that whip around an empty-looking point at tremendous speeds. From those orbits, they worked out that the invisible object must have the mass of about four million suns, packed into a tiny region. Nothing else we know of fits that description. A second clue is X-rays. When a black hole pulls gas off a neighboring star, that gas doesn't drop straight in. It spirals, and as it does, it heats up to millions of degrees and glows in X-rays. And then there's a much more recent method, which, frankly, still gives me goosebumps. In twenty fifteen, detectors picked up gravitational waves, tiny ripples in space itself, produced when two black holes collided more than a billion years ago. So notice the pattern here. In none of these cases are we observing the black hole directly. We're inferring it, the way you'd infer wind from the movement of the trees.

### Academic talk - Philosophy (Questions 44-47)

[F-US] I want to start today with a ship. According to the ancient writer Plutarch, the people of Athens kept the ship of their hero Theseus for centuries as a kind of monument. Wood rots, of course, so whenever a plank decayed, they swapped in a new one. Now suppose that, in the end, not a single original plank is left. Is it still the ship of Theseus? Hands up if you say yes. Hmm, most of you. OK. Now let me spoil your confidence. In the seventeenth century, Thomas Hobbes added a twist. Suppose somebody had quietly collected every old plank as it was removed, and then put them all back together. Now there are two ships. Which one is the real one? If you think a thing is just its material, you have to pick the rebuilt pile of old wood. If you think what matters is a continuous history, an unbroken story, you'll stay with the ship that was repaired. And notice, this isn't really about boats. Many of the cells in your body have been replaced since you were a child, and yet you'd insist you're the same person. I'm not going to hand you a solution, by the way. Philosophers have been arguing over this one for two thousand years, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who tells you it's settled.
